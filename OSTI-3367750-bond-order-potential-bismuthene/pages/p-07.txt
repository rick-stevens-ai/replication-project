Modeling the Crystal Lattice Structure, Cohesive Energy,
Equation of State, Elastic Constants, and Phonon

Figure 3a provides a detailed comparison between the phonon
dispersion predicted by the ML-Tersoff model and the
reference DFT calculations. The ML-Tersoff potential
reproduces the overall structure of the phonon branches,
capturing both the low-frequency acoustic modes and the
higher-frequency optical modes with reasonable fidelity. As
expected from its slightly stiffer elastic response (Table 2), the
longitudinal acoustic (LA) and transverse acoustic (TA)
branches show a modest upward shift relative to DFT,
particularly near the I’ point and along the high-symmetry
directions. This upward scaling reflects the increased curvature
of the interatomic potential near equilibrium, which directly
translates into larger acoustic velocities and stiffer restoring
forces. In two-dimensional materials such as bismuthene, the
acoustic branches especially the ZA flexural mode play a
dominant role in thermal transport, so even modest stiffening
of the LA and TA modes has measurable consequences for the
predicted thermal conductivity, as discussed later.

To further evaluate vibrational accuracy, we analyze the
phonon density of states (DOS), also shown in Figure 3b. The
ML-Tersoff model reproduces the overall density of states,
capturing the correct separation between acoustic and optical
branches. The DOS retains the correct weighting of states at
low and intermediate frequencies, indicating that the
integrated phonon spectrum remains consistent with DFT
even though individual mode frequencies are slightly shifted.
This is further supported by the specific heat prediction
(C(T)) in Figure 3c, where the ML-Tersoff heat-capacity
curve nearly overlaps with DFT across the entire temperature
range. Accurate prediction of C,(T) suggests that the ML-
Tersoff potential correctly captures the thermal characteristics
of phonon modes and therefore represents the vibrational free
energy landscape of bismuthene reliably.

The equation of state (EOS) in Figure 3d provides an
additional measure of the potential’s accuracy in describing the
energetic landscape under strain. The ML-Tersoff's EOS curve
matches the DFT reference closely over both tensile and
compressive strains, accurately capturing the equilibrium
volume, cohesive energy, and curvature near zero strain.
Because the curvature of the EOS is proportional to the elastic

constants, this agreement is consistent with the moderate 573

underprediction of C,,, Cy,, and Cgg reported in Table 2.

As discussed earlier, the equilibrium bond-order values at
(ro, 9) are highest for ML-Tersoff-2 (b, = 0.943),
intermediate for ML-Tersoff-1 (b; * 0.938), and lowest for
ML-Tersoff (bj * 0.935). Because the bond-order term
reduces the strength of the attractive interaction as the
coordination increases, a larger bond-order value corresponds
to a weaker effective bond. This explains why ML-Tersoff-2
produces the softest elastic constants and phonon modes, as
well as the largest fracture strain discussed later. Conversely,
ML-Tersoff-1, with its smaller bond-order value, maintains
stronger effective bond strengths, resulting in higher elastic
constants, stiffer acoustic modes, and a reduced fracture strain.

These combined insights reveal a coherent picture: the three
RL-optimized potentials represent distinct solutions within the
broader Tersoff parameter landscape, each embodying a
different balance among pairwise stiffness, angular rigidity,
and coordination-dependent bond weakening. ML-Tersoff-1
yields the stiffest bonding environment, ML-Tersoff produces a
balanced intermediate behavior, and ML-Tersoff-2 corre-
sponds to the softest bonding landscape. As shown in the
subsequent sections, these bonding differences directly trans-
late into variations in thermal conductivity, mechanical fracture
response, Griineisen parameters, and modal analysis.
