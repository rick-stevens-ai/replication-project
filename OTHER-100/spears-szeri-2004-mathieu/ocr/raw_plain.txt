Topology and resonances in a quasiperiodically forced oscillator
Brian K. Spears, Andrew J. Szeri∗
Mechanical Engineering, University of California, Berkeley, CA 94720-1740, USA
Received 10 November 2003; received in revised form 21 April 2004; accepted 17 June 2004
Communicated by C.K.R.T. Jones

Abstract
In this paper we analyze resonance behavior in a quasiperiodically forced, nonlinear Mathieu equation. We develop a perturbation technique based on the method of multiple scales to find both a criterion for resonance and approximations to solutions
in the neighborhood of a resonance. We compare the perturbation results to numerical solutions to validate both the resonance
criterion and the approximate solutions. We also investigate the implications of resonance for the topology of attractors in the
four-dimensional phase space. We show that a resonance occurs due to topological torus bifurcations (TTBs) and that resonant
trajectories lie on topologically interesting knotted tori we have recently described elsewhere (Topological bifurcations of attracting 2-tori of quasiperiodically driven nonlinear oscillators, in review). The perturbation approximations capture both TTBs
and the topology of invariant manifolds near resonance.

1. Introduction
We study in this paper a quasiperiodic (QP) Mathieu equation with damping and cubic nonlinearities. The
behavior of similar equations has been studied by a number of researchers in recent years. Stability and transition
curves for a linear, undamped QP Mathieu equation were studied by Zounes and Rand [1] using a variety of
methods including perturbation. A perturbation method was also employed by Alfred et al. [2] to study resonances
in a QP linear Mathieu equation for the motions of a charged particle in a quadrupole ion trap. Zounes and Rand also
investigated subharmonic resonances and chaos in a nonlinear QP Mathieu equation [3,4]. Guennoun and coworkers
developed approaches for generating approximations to solutions of QP Mathieu equations with weak damping and

bifurcation through which a torus changes its knot type. We also discuss the topology of attracting tori, saddle-type

At O(1 ), the differential equation is the same linear Mathieu equation as at O(0 ), now driven by functions of z0
and its derivatives. Driving terms appear in (7) through the contributions of the weak damping, nonlinearity, and
secondary excitation. To ensure asymptoticness of the solution up to times of order 1/2 , one normally enforces
solvability conditions at O(1 ) to remove all inhomogeneous terms that cause secular growth of the z1 solution—
assuming these should not be present. In a more typical example, one would solve a sequence of simple oscillator
problems with a single natural frequency. In that context, the pair of slowly varying amplitudes A and B are sufficient
to remove all secular terms at O(1 ).
However, the present situation is considerably more complicated. Here we are solving an inhomogeneous Mathieu
equation at O(1 ). Consequently, we must carefully analyze which external forcing frequencies cause secularity.
Kotowski [12] shows that a Mathieu equation with homogeneous solution (8) subjected to periodic external forcing
with frequency ωe :

has unbounded oscillations only when ωe = 2n + β(α, γ), with β given by (9). These resonant solutions of the
externally forced Mathieu equation grow linearly in time.
In the present context, terms on the right-hand-side of (7) play the roles of F cos ωe t in (14) with F depending
on the coefficients D2n from z0 . Each such resonant term causes growth. However, as n increases, the slope of
the linearly growing envelope is proportional to D2n , where D2n is the coefficient of the term in the homogeneous
solution whose frequency matches the forcing frequency. Thus, although resonances for all n grow linearly in time,
those with smaller n are much more dangerous in the sense of destroying asymptoticness of the expansion earlier. In
the context of our current perturbation scheme, we therefore choose to enforce solvability conditions which remove
only the most dangerous resonant terms from the right-hand-side of (7). Because we have introduced only two
slowly varying amplitudes, A(τ) and B(τ), we can eliminate only the sine and cosine functions containing a single
resonant forcing frequency.

2.2. Resonance conditions
It can be observed through numerical experiments that solutions to (2) decay to zero, except for a set of special ωf
values, which we call resonant values. For these exceptional ωf , steady oscillations with large amplitudes occur. Our
goal at this point is to find values of ωf that cause large-amplitude solutions. The multiple scales method described
in Section 2.1 captures both the decaying solutions and the resonant ones. The essential difference between these

in (2) occur when the special values of ωf introduce secular terms at O(1 ) which are absent for arbitrarily chosen
ωf . A more detailed examination of the right-hand-side of (7) reveals the special set of frequencies ωf that drive

Numerical simulations confirm that solutions for predicted resonant values of ωf and those for values of ωf
sufficiently far from resonance are qualitatively different. Far from resonance, the dissipation drives solutions to
zero exponentially fast, regardless of initial conditions3 . At the resonant values predicted by the multiple scales
analysis, open sets of initial conditions are attracted to knotted tori. Figs. 1–3 illustrate these distinct types of
behavior in the time series. We consider the geometry and topology of the associated attractors in Section 3. We
note that while resonant secondary frequencies ωf = p + β with p = 0 cause resonance, these higher resonances
are more delicate. That is, we must provide strong secondary excitation and weak damping in order to observe
them in numerical simulations. Consequently, for the remainder of the paper we will concentrate on the more robust
resonance when ωf = β, which we call the central resonance.

As noted earlier, the D2n decrease in magnitude rapidly for increasing n. Removal of the secular driving terms
with frequency β eliminates the fastest growing secular terms which grow in proportion to D0 . This leaves a variety
of secular terms, the strongest of which grows in proportion to D−2 . Thus, the method should remain asymptotic
for times tm = O(1/D−2 ). In practice, this requires that α and γ be chosen to make |D−2 | small in order to ensure
asymptoticness for sufficiently long times.
We show in Figs. 4 and 5 both numerical solutions and perturbation approximations at the central resonance for an
(α, γ) pair that makes D−2 = −0.07. The perturbation approximation captures the high-frequency oscillation on the
fast time scale, as well as the slow evolution of the envelope. The dynamics of the system of slowly varying amplitude
equations is best understood by examining slow-time solution trajectories in the (A, B)-plane. At resonance, the
trajectories in this space spiral into a stable, attracting fixed point (Fig. 6). The presence of this stable focus together
with the complicated solution on the fast time scale leads to knotted torus attractors whose topology will be discussed
below.
For comparison, we also show here perturbation approximations and numerical solutions (see Figs. 7–9) for an
(α, γ) pair that makes D−2 = −0.11, an increase from the previous case. This results in a less accurate approximation
to the solution. The approximation still captures the qualitative behavior and also reveals the approach to the attractor
for long times. It also captures the high frequency “ripple” behavior on the fast time scale. However, the increased
strength of remaining secular terms introduces error. As can be seen in Fig. 7, the perturbation approximation
slightly overestimates the amplitude of the solution envelope. The errors are larger for the transient stage of the
solution (Fig. 8), but are decreased at steady state (Fig. 9). The method still correctly predicts the presence of an
attractor due to a stable fixed point in the (A, B)-plane.

3. Bifurcations and topology of solutions
3.1. Solutions near the central resonance
Both examples in Section 2.3 (Figs. 4–6 and 7–9, respectively) demonstrate that steady, large amplitude solutions can occur. While the approximation scheme yields both the resonant frequencies and the resonant solutions, it remains to be shown how the attractor is engendered. The method of multiple scales, as constructed,
does not give information about the bifurcations that lead to the birth and death of the large-amplitude attractor. Additional insight about the bifurcations leading to resonant solutions can be gained by studying the
behavior in an interval of secondary frequencies that surrounds the resonant frequency. This can be done
by including detuning in the multiple scales scheme. We define the secondary frequency of (2) to have the

3.2. Topological bifurcations of resonant solutions

occur for ωf near 0.6375 and 0.6405. At the lower frequency, an unstable branch (not shown) bifurcates from the
stable trivial branch, which becomes unstable thereafter. As ωf is increased across the higher frequency, the stable
large-amplitude branch meets the unstable trivial solution and stabilizes it. The behavior of the associated attractors
indicated by the response diagram is best understood using a topological approach.

In [8] we show that the change in knot type at a TTB of knotted torus attractors in the full phase space is captured
by this pair of maps. Such bifurcations can be observed by examining the topology of invariant 1-manifolds that
exist in the Poincaré sections defined above. These 1-manifolds have the form of (closed) braids. For the present
purposes, we need only recall that that the number of strands in a braid is equal to the degree of the torus attractor,
and the degree is an isotopy invariant which can change only at a bifurcation.
With this in mind, we first consider the braids present in the Poincaré sections shown in Fig. 16 for solutions on
the stable portions of the trivial branch in the response diagram. Here, the decay to the trivial solution is reflected
in the transient approach to the long term attractor in the cross-sections. The simple topology of the attractor, a
single strand braid, is faithfully reproduced by the multiple scales approximation. For initial conditions leading to
solutions on the large-amplitude stable branch, trajectories approach the attractor shown in the Poincaré sections
of Fig. 17. The braids associated with this attractor are both 2-strand braids. As mentioned before, the number of
strands is equal to the degree of the surface and is isotopy invariant. Thus, this torus attractor in the full phase space
R2 × T 2 has different knot type from the torus associated with the single strand braid. These torus attractors of
different knot type are introduced to and eliminated from the phase space through TTBs.
The bifurcation at ωf ≃ 0.6405 is a type-II TTB of the doubling variety [8]. By this we mean that as ωf is reduced
across the bifurcation value, the trivial single strand braids in the two Poincaré sections on the trivial branch lose
stability and shed stable two strand braids. In the section Σ θ10 the number of components (one) remains constant
while the number of components doubles to two in Σ θ20 . This topological transition can be tracked by a series of
numerical simulations and is also captured by the detuned multiple scales approximation. The TTB appears in the
multiple scales approximation as a period-doubling in the slow-time evolution equations (Fig. 18). The bifurcation
at ωf ≃ 0.6375 is a similar bifurcation with the stability roles reversed. It is also captured by the multiple scales
calculations.

4. Conclusions
The damped, nonlinear Mathieu equation with two-frequency parametric forcing supports large-amplitude resonant oscillations for appropriately chosen secondary forcing frequencies, ωf . A variant of the method of multiple
scales reveals the resonant values of ωf and approximations to resonant solutions. The introduction of detuning
enables the capture of a pair of period doubling bifurcations in the slow-time equations which generate the resonant
peak in the response diagram. By adopting a topological approach, it is shown that these bifurcations are type-II
TTBs of the doubling variety, that we described in an earlier work [8]. The detuned perturbation approximations near
resonance capture the topology of solutions in the R2 × T 2 phase space. They provide a closed form description of
trajectories on knotted 2-tori in the four-dimensional phase space. Finally, analysis of the slow-time Poincaré map
composed with itself provides approximations for the location of saddle-type invariant tori and their stable invariant
3-manifolds which form basin boundaries in R2 × T 2 and organize the phase space.
These results help clarify the nature of solutions which densely cover knotted tori. That is, flow on a knotted torus
near resonance is characterized by the presence of multiple frequencies, including the several frequencies from the

B.K. Spears, A.J. Szeri / Physica D 197 (2004) 69–85

85

fast dynamics and the frequency of the slow, periodic amplitudes. While solutions with many frequencies could not
be captured by a traditional multiple scales routine that admits only single-frequency fast dynamics, our technique
captures the multiple frequencies and the associated topology. It also makes clear that there is a fundamental relation
between the slow dynamics, the fast dynamics, and the knot type of the resulting attractor. However, it is worth
emphasizing that our work here has not elucidated the exact nature of this relation. For example, why does a pair
of type II TTBs occur, as opposed to a pair of type I? Is the number of components in the sections Σ θi0 controlled
by the fast dynamics, the slow dynamics, or an interaction between the two? The techniques developed here should
help to answer these and related questions in future investigations.
