Abstract. Deinococcus radiodurans is an extremophilic microorganism renowned for its
extraordinary resistance to ionizing radiation. Its unique DNA repair capacity and robust
protein protection mechanisms make it an important model organism for studying
cellular responses to radiation stress. Although multiple radiation-resistance
mechanisms have been elucidated, proteomic-level insights—particularly regarding
proteins speciOically expressed after irradiation—remain limited. In this study, we
employed high-resolution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-
MS/MS) combined with bioinformatic analysis to systematically investigate the
proteomic proOiles of D. radiodurans at 0, 1, and 3 hours following exposure to 6 kGy of γ-
radiation. Unlike traditional differential expression analysis, this study focused on
proteins exclusively identiOied in the irradiated group, from which a total of 62
irradiation-induced proteins were identiOied.Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis
revealed that these proteins are predominantly involved in essential biological processes
such as DNA repair, DNA binding, and cellular response to gamma radiation. Further
analysis showed that irradiation-induced DNA repair proteins—RuvC, DdrA, and DdrB—
exhibited a time-dependent increase in peptide-spectrum match (PSM) counts between 0
and 3 hours post-irradiation, indicating their critical roles in responding to radiation-
induced genomic damage. These Oindings provide new proteomic evidence for
understanding microbial radioresistance and offer promising molecular targets for
applications in space biology and radiation protection technologies.
1. Introduction
Deinococcus radiodurans is one of the most radiation- and oxidation-resistant
microorganisms known to date. It is capable of maintaining genomic and proteomic integrity
under extreme conditions such as high-dose ionizing radiation and intense oxidative stress,
thereby exhibiting remarkable environmental adaptability[1–4]. This extraordinary resistance
makes it a powerful model organism for investigating radiation damage response mechanisms,
developing radiation protection strategies, and exploring the potential for life in extraterrestrial
environments. To date, numerous studies have focused on the DNA repair mechanisms [5–8] and
antioxidant enzyme systems [1,9–11] of D. radiodurans. However, relatively few investigations
have explored the proteins that are speciKically induced after irradiation—i.e., proteins absent
from unirradiated controls—and their functional roles in stress response.
To address this gap, the present study employs high-resolution liquid chromatography–
tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) coupled with bioinformatic analysis to systematically
characterize the proteomic proKiles of D. radiodurans at 0, 1, and 3 hours following exposure to 6The Second International Conference on Space Science and Technology

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Strains and Culture Conditions
The strain Deinococcus radiodurans (catalog number CGMCC 1.633) was sourced from the
China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC, Beijing, China). Bacterial cells
were propagated in TGY broth comprising 1% tryptone, 0.5% glucose, and 0.1% yeast extract.
Cultivation was carried out at 30°C under constant shaking at 150rpm.
2.2. Irradiation Conditions and Postirradiation Recovery
Bacterialculturesweregrownat30 °Cand150rpmuntiltheyreachedearlystationaryphase
(OD₆₀₀ ≈ 1.5). Subsequently, they were exposed to ⁶⁰Co γ-radiation at a cumulative dose of 6kGy
(dose rate: 30Gy/min) at Peking University (Beijing, China). Parallel cultures that were not
irradiated served as controls.
Afterirradiation,bothtreatedandcontrolsampleswerecentrifuged(10,000×g,5min,4°C),
then resuspended in fresh TGY medium to a starting OD₆₀₀ of 0.1. Cells were allowed to recover
at 30°C and 150rpm, and samples were taken at 0, 1, 3, 6, and 12hours post-irradiation. Each
sample underwent two PBS washes, followed by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10min, 4°C). All
procedures were performed in three independent biological replicates.
2.3. Protein Extraction and Digestion
Harvested cell pellets were lysed using a buffer containing 8M urea, 2mM EDTA, and 1mM
PMSF. Lysis was facilitated via probe sonication on ice (VCX800, 800W, 20kHz, pulse mode: 2s
on/2s off for 4min). The lysates were clariKied by centrifugation (15,000 × g, 45min, 4°C), and
protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (Pierce, MA, USA).
For enzymatic digestion, 50μg of protein from each sample was reduced with 10mM DTT at
56°C for 30minutes, then alkylated in the dark using 50mM IAA at room temperature for
30minutes. To lower the urea concentration below 2M, samples were diluted with 50mM
ammonium bicarbonate. Trypsin digestion was performed overnight (enzyme-to-substrate ratio
of 1:50, w/w) at 37°C, followed by an additional 4-hour digestion at a 1:100 ratio.
The reaction was terminated using 1% formic acid (v/v). Resulting peptides were desalted
via Monospin C18 cartridges (Shimadzu, Japan), vacuum-dried, and stored at –80°C for further
analysis.
2.4. NanoLC–MS/MS Analysis
Dried peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10min)
before LC–MS/MS analysis. Peptides were analyzed using an Easy-nLC 1000 system coupled to a
Q-ExactiveHFXmassspectrometer(ThermoFisherScientiKic,Bremen,Germany).Separationwas
achieved using a homemade C18 reverse-phase column (100μm ID × 15cm, 1.9μm resin), using

2.6. Bioinformatic Analysis
Proteins identiKied using pFind3 were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional
enrichment analysis using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8, including the categories of
biological process (GO_BP), cellular component (GO_CC), and molecular function (GO_MF). The
enrichment threshold was set with an EASE score cutoff of 0.05 and a minimum count of 2 for
each GO term. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using Python, and Kigures
were created with BioRender (https://BioRender.com).

4 Conclusion
In this study, high-resolution mass spectrometry combined with bioinformatic analysis was
employed to systematically characterize the proteomic changes in Deinococcus radiodurans
within 0–3 hours after exposure to 6 kGy of γ-radiation. Particular emphasis was placed on
proteins that were exclusively expressed in the irradiated group, revealing their involvement in
DNA repair and radiation response pathways. Although a large number of core proteins were
shared between the control and irradiated samples, radiation treatment speciKically induced the
expression of 62 unique proteins. GO enrichment analysis showed that these proteins were
signiKicantly associated with functional categories such as "DNA repair," "DNA binding," and
"cellular response to gamma radiation." Further analysis revealed that key DNA repair proteins—
RuvC, DdrA, and DdrB—accumulated progressively during the recovery period and were
exclusively detected in the irradiated group, indicating their essential roles in the rapid response
to radiation-induced damage and the maintenance of genome integrity. Overall, these Kindings
enhance our understanding of the molecular basis underlying the extraordinary radioresistance
of D. radiodurans, and provide novel insights into microbial survival strategies, radiation
protection, and space biology.The Second International Conference on Space Science and Technology
