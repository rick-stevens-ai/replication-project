        Abstract

        The increasing sophistication of Monte Carlo (MC)-based simulations, such as TOPAS-nBio for radiation track structure and DNA damage, and MEDRAS-MC for subsequent DNA repair and cell survival, has ushered in an era of in-silico single-cell radiobiology. However, these simulations remain computationally prohibitive. In this study, we introduce a novel method that significantly accelerates the integrated simulation process for in-silico single-cell radiobiology. Our approach centers on pre-calculating and constructing a single-particle-track (SPT) standard DNA damage (SDD) data library using TOPAS-nBio. Each SPT-SDD data entry in this library records the positions of various DNA damage types (e.g. base damage, single-strand breaks, and double-strand breaks) produced by a single particle track. The comprehensive library contains a large number of SPT-SDD data entries, covering a broad range of particle energies. This data library functions as a look-up table, allowing for rapid assembly of DNA damage data for any desired dose level. This is achieved by randomly fetching and superimposing many SPT-SDD data entries from the pre-calculated library. The resulting ‘composite’ SDD data file then serves as input for MEDRAS-MC to simulate and compute DNA damage outcomes, including chromosome aberrations and cell lethality for each individual cell. Furthermore, ‘timestamps’ can be added to the superimposed data to account for dose rate effects. This work presents the first integrated MC framework that bridges track-structure simulation (TOPAS-nBio) with mechanistic repair–misrepair modeling (MEDRAS-MC) to predict chromosome aberrations and cell lethality on a cell-by-cell basis. In this paper, we first describe this novel methodology and then apply it to compute radiation-induced outcomes for three previously reported in vitro experiments. These applications involve 280 kVp x-rays, protons, and alpha particles, covering a broad spectrum of linear energy transfer. Finally, we compare our in silico results with experimental data and provide a detailed discussion of any observed discrepancies.

        1. Introduction

        Recent advances in cellular radiobiology modeling and simulation have led to the development of increasingly accessible computational tools. These include Monte Carlo track-structure (MCTS) codes (Kreipl et al 2009, Bernal et al 2015, Plante et al 2019), which simulate individual radiation tracks traversing a cell nucleus with nanometer-scale detail. Among current MCTS platforms, the open-source TOPAS-nBio package (Schuemann et al 2019b)—extension of the Geant4-DNA toolkit (Incerti et al 2010)—is one of the most widely adopted for radiobiological research. It provides detailed modeling of the physical and chemical processes induced by radiation tracks and reports comprehensive information on initial DNA damage, including damage types and spatial locations within the cell nucleus.

        In parallel, a number of biological response models have been developed to describe the kinetics of DNA damage repair and misrepair and to translate initial DNA damage into cellular endpoints (Ballarini and Carante 2016, Warmenhoven et al 2020, McMahon et al 2021). The introduction of the standard DNA damage (SDD) format (Schuemann et al 2019a) has enabled interoperability between track-structure simulations and downstream biological models. This has facilitated integrated frameworks in which DNA damage produced by MCTS codes such as TOPAS-nBio can be directly coupled to mechanistic repair models, a prominent example being the open-source Mechanistic DNA Repair and Survival (MEDRAS) model (McMahon and Prise 2021). Together, these approaches enable simulations of double-strand break (DSB) repair and misrepair under complex irradiation conditions and allow prediction of experimentally measurable biological endpoints such as chromosome aberrations and cell survival.

        Such coupled track-structure-biology frameworks have broad applications, including investigation of relative biological effectiveness (RBE) across radiation qualities (e.g. low- versus high-LET radiation) and evaluation of temporal effects such as high-dose-rate versus low-dose-rate irradiation. By explicitly modeling radiation-induced processes at the subcellular scale, these methods offer greater mechanistic insight and predictive potential than phenomenological models such as the linear-quadratic model. However, two major challenges continue to limit their widespread application. First, MCTS simulations that include detailed radiochemistry and DNA damage modeling remain computationally expensive. Second, biological predictions derived from these frameworks have not yet been systematically validated against a broad range of experimental data.

        To address existing computational bottlenecks, this study introduces an efficient methodology—integrating TOPAS-nBio and MEDRAS to enable cell-by-cell simulations of DNA damage, chromosome aberrations, and cell survival. Our approach circumvents prohibitive ‘brute-force’ MCTS calculations by utilizing a pre-calculated data library containing single-particle track (SPT) DNA damage data in the SDD format. While we previously demonstrated this concept for limited electron (beta particle) irradiation (Lim et al 2023), the current work expands this simulation method and makes it applicable to a broad spectrum of irradiation scenarios including both low- and high-LET radiation.

        Section 2 details the construction of the SPT damage database (SDD) and outlines the methodology for efficiently retrieving data entries to simulate specific irradiation scenarios. These simulations are applied to three previously reported in vitro experiments involving 280 kVp x-rays (Cornforth et al 1987), protons (Marshall et al 2016), and alpha-particles (Inkret et al 1990, Eisen et al 1991, Raju et al 1991, Cornforth et al 2002). Subsequently, section 3 presents the simulated biological outcomes and their comparison with experimental data. Finally, section 4 provides a discussion of the observed discrepancies and suggests directions for future model development.

        2. Method

        The cornerstone of this new method is a comprehensive SPT SDD data library. This pre-calculated data library was generated using TOPAS-nBio (v4.0), with results formatted according to the SDD standards established by Schuemann et al (2019a). The library was constructed through extensive simulations conducted over several months using the high-performance computing clusters at ORNL (CADES) and Georgia Tech (Phoenix).

        The framework is built upon two primary assumptions:

        • (1)  
          During irradiation, charged particles are assumed to traverse the cell nucleus with random directional orientation. This is justified by the fact that cells and their subcellular components (such as the nucleus and chromosomes) are randomly oriented relative to the incident radiation field.
        • (2)  
          Radiation-induced cellular outcomes, namely chromosome aberrations and cell death, are assumed to result solely from the misrepair of initial DNA damage, specifically DSBs, inside the cell nucleus. Lethal events resulting from other mechanisms, such as senescence and bystander effects, are excluded from the current model. This mechanistic assumption allows for a highly efficient simulation approach: using a pre-calculated SPT-SDD library to rapidly assemble dose-specific composite SDD files. Each file contains the initial DNA damage profile for an individual cell irradiated at a prescribed dose and serve as the primary input for the MEDRAS-MC code to predict the outcomes for that cell.

        These two assumptions together made it possible to build a highly efficient workflow for simulating and predicting various radiation-induced cellular outcomes (DNA misrepairs, chromosome aberrations, and cell survival fractions) across a wide range of irradiation scenarios on cell-by-cell basis.

        Section 2.1 below describes the organization of the SPT-SDD data library and how it is generated. Section 2.2 outlines how the MEDRAS-MC code utilizes ‘initial DNA damages’ (recorded in the SDD data format) to simulate DNA damage repair and misrepair, resulting in lethal chromosome aberrations and cell death on a cell-by-cell basis. Following this, section 2.3 details the three-step process of the novel simulation method and show how it can be applied to simulate three previously conducted in vitro irradiation scenarios.

        2.1. SPT-SDD data library generation

        We have generated three distinct SPT-SDD data libraries, categorized by particle type: electron, proton, and alpha particle. Each data library is composed of extensive, energy-dependent look-up tables, where each table contains a multitude of SPT-SDD data entries specific to the particle’s entry energy into the cell nucleus. To ensure statistical rigor, the aggregate data within each table represents a cumulative dose exceeding 100 Gy. The high-volume data minimizes the probability of retrieving the same DNA damage data multiple times during a single simulation and ensures that each simulated irradiation scenario is composed of distinct, non-repetitive damage histories. The electron data library covers an energy range of 1 keV–1 MeV. The proton library covers an energy range of 50 keV–100 MeV. The alpha particle library covers an energy range of 0.1–10 MeV. As shown in table 1, the full energy range for each particle library is further divided into several subranges, and the energy increment (ΔE) separating two adjacent energy points within each subrange varies. For the electron library, ΔE increases with electron energy: 1 keV (1–100 keV), 2 keV (100–300 keV), 5 keV (300–500 keV), and 10 keV (500–1 MeV). Similarly, ΔE also increases as the energy increases for the proton and alpha particle libraries. The ΔE values were chosen to ensure that the particle’s stopping powers (or LET) of two adjacent energy points differ by less than 10%, thus maintaining similar particle track structures.

        The cell nucleus modeled in the TOPAS-nBio runs was that of a human fibroblast nucleus at G0/G1 phase built within the TOPAS-nBio framework and was the same as that described by Zhu et al (2020a). Briefly, the nucleus has a spherical shape with a diameter of 9.3 μm and 46 chromosomes. The subnuclear structures were organized in a hierarchical order including the chromosome territories, chromatin fibers, nucleosomes and DNA double helix. The size of each chromosome, measured in base pairs, was based on the Genome Reference Consortium (GRC) data (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/grc).

        Chromatin fibers were folded according to a continuous three-dimensional Hilbert space-filling curve to generate chromatin fiber loops within individual voxels. After accounting for the spherical nuclear boundary, the full nucleus model comprised 14 328 DNA-containing voxels that corresponding to 6.08 Gbp approximately. The length of each chromatin fiber consist of 120 nm and follow a left-handed solenoidal rule with six nucleosomes per turn. Each nucleosome consist with a cylindrical histone core with a diameter of 6.6 nm and a height of 5.7 nm. DNA-double helix was constructed from repeated base and backbone subvolumes and placed along a helical trajectory around the histone axis. Consecutive base pairs were seperated by an axial rise of 0.34 nm and rotated by a constant angular offset increment of 36°, which corresponds to 10 base pairs per helical turn. Individual base pairs were represented as ellipsoid with seimi-axes a = b = 0.328 nm and c = 0.185 nm and the sugar-phosphate backbone was modeled as spherical volume with a radius of 0.271 nm. Additionally, a partial spherical hydration shell with a thickness of 0.16 nm was externally placed around the backbone.

        In a TOPAS-nBio run, each particle track starts with a randomly selected position on the cell nucleus surface. The particle’s initial traveling direction is randomly selected in an isotropic manner inwardly towards the nucleus. The resulting single particle track data file includes the energy deposition by the particle in the cell nucleus and the DNA damage sites produced by the track in the SDD format. The physics module recommended for use in water radiolysis, TsEmDNAPhysics, was used for physical stage simulation. Direct strand breaks were scored using the TOPAS-nBio spherical DNA damage model, in which a strand break is induced when sufficient energy is deposited within the DNA backbone volume and its neighboring hydration shell. Chemical interactions in the pre-chemical and chemical stages were simulated using TsEmDNAChemistry, with the chemical stage length set to 1 ns following Lampe et al (2018).

        Indirect DNA damage was modeled using a probabilistic scavenging-and-reaction framework consistent with the spherical DNA model. While all radiolysis species were tracked, only hydroxyl radical (•OH) interactions with DNA were assumed to induce indirect strand breaks, following previously reported approaches (Meylan et al 2017, Sakata et al 2019). In addition, quasi-direct damage via charge transfer from the hydration shell to the DNA backbone was included. DSBs were defined as two strand breaks occurring on opposite strands within a specified base-pair separation. All DNA damage scoring parameters followed the TOPAS-nBio spherical DNA model recommendations and are summarized in the S.2.

The program then records all repair and misrepair events, along with the cell’s final state, thus completing the DSB repair/misrepair simulation in MEDRAS for that cell.

        For protracted exposures, which are most relevant to low-LET radiation, the simulation follows a similar process. The program begins by sorting the input SDD data file to identify each SPT SDD data set containing at least one DSB. Each identified data set is then assigned a ‘timepoint’ marking when the DSB(s) occurred. The timepoint is determined by calculating the average time interval between two consecutive DSB-containing SPT SDD data sets.

        For instance, consider a protracted low-LET irradiation scenario delivering a total dose of 5 Gy over 5 h, containing 9000 individual single-electron-track SDD data entries. In this scenario, the time interval between two consecutive electron tracks would be 0.5 s. The program adds this timepoint to the original SDD data file, reflecting each DSB’s arrival time. If a new DSB is predicted to occur before the next DSB repair event, that new DSB is incorporated into the simulation. Consequently, and are updated for all DSBs to account for the newly available rejoining partners.

        2.3. Simulation of in-vitro irradiation experiments using the pre-calculated SPT-SDD data libraries

        A significant challenge in radiobiological modeling is the complexity of real-world irradiation, where each cell nucleus is exposed to a diverse spectrum of charged particles across broad energy ranges. To overcome this challenge, we developed a versatile simulation method applicable to a wide range of irradiation scenarios. By leveraging a pre-calculated SPT-SDD data library, our approach efficiently models the mechanistic details of DNA damage and the subsequent repair or misrepair processes. It also enables the prediction of chromosome aberrations and cell lethality on cell-by-cell basis through the three-step integrated process illustrated in figure 1 and detailed below:

        • Step 1: macro-scale radiation transport calculation using TOPAS—we utilized OpenTOPAS version 4.0 (Perl et al 2012, Faddegon et al 2020), a MC radiation transport code, to model the physical irradiation setup. This step determines the unique charged-particle spectrum to which individual cell nuclei are exposed for each specific irradiation scenario.
        • Step 2: dose-specific construction of the composite SDD data files—the charged-particle spectrum from Step 1 is converted into a cumulative distribution function (CDF). Using a MC sampling approach, the CDF is used with a specialized dose-matching algorithm and a random number to retrieve and assemble specific data entries from the SPT-SDD library. Each retrieved entry contains the particle’s energy deposition within the cell nucleus along with the spatial coordinates of the resulting DNA lesions. Depending on the prescribed dose and the particle’s LET, each assembled ‘composite’ SDD data file (representing the total DNA lesion profile for that individual cell) may contain a few or a multitude of SPT-SDD data entries retrieved from the SDD data library. This step needs to be repeated on cell-by-cell basis for at least 10 000 cells to ensure statistical accuracy for each prescribed dose point.
        • Step 3: prediction of chromosome aberrations and cell lethality using MEDRAS-MC—The composite SDD file from Step 2 for each cell serves as the input for the MEDRAS-MC code to simulate the DNA repair and misrepair kinetic and to predict chromosome aberrations and cell lethality. Same as in Step 2, this step also needs to be repeated on cell-by-cell basis for at least 10 000 cells to ensure statistical accuracy for each prescribed dose point.

        The simulation workflow employs a pipelined execution model to maximize computational efficiency. A set of assigned dose points is defined at the beginning of the simulation. Once the SDD data construction (Step 2) for a specific dose point is completed, the system immediately proceeds to the next assigned dose point. Simultaneously, the MEDRAS-MC calculation (Step 3) for the previously completed dose batch begins in parallel. This overlapping process is repeated across all assigned dose points until the final dose point is completed, resulting in the dose-response curves for chromosome aberrations and cell survival.

        To demonstrate the universal applicability of the simulation method, we use the following examples of three distinct in vitro irradiation scenarios representing a wide spectrum of linear energy transfer (LET):

        • Low-LET: 280 kVp x-rays.
        • Intermediate-LET: Protons at the distal edge of a clinical proton beam.
        • High-LET: 238Pu alpha particles.

        Each of the three simulations described below closely follows the three-step process described above.

        2.3.1. Simulation of in-vitro cells irradiated with 280 kVp x-rays

        In Step 1, we used TOPAS to simulate the in-vitro 280 kVp x-ray irradiation experiment. The simulation geometry included air, a 2 mm-thick Petri dish, and a soft tissue region (modeled as water) to represent the cell layer and medium (figure 2). Using a SpekCalc-generated photon spectrum (Poludniowski et al 2009), 108 photons were emitted isotropically from a point source 10 cm below the dish. The resulting electron spectrum, tallied as they entered the cell layer from the dish, provided the statistically converged energy histogram shown in figure 3.

        In Step 2, the electron energy histogram (figure 3) was converted to a cumulative distribution function (CDF) to facilitate MC sampling. By applying a random number to this CDF, a specific electron energy was selected to retrieve the corresponding entry from the pre-calculated SPT-SDD data library. Each retrieved dataset provides the electron’s energy deposition within the cell nucleus along and the 3D coordinates of the resulting DNA lesions. This data retrieval and assembly process was iterated until the accumulated energy deposition from all selected tracks matched the specified dose. The resulting composite SDD data file serves as a high-fidelity representation of the total DNA damage profile for an individual cell nucleus. To ensure statistical accuracy, this cell-by-cell process was repeated for a batch of 10 000 cells for each prescribed dose point.

        In Step 3, the composite SDD files were processed using MEDRAS-MC to simulate the kinetics of DSB repair and misrepair This stage predicts the formation of chromosome aberrations and subsequent cell lethality, which were evaluated at 24 h post-irradiation. A cell was classified as dead if it contained at least one lethal chromosome aberration, defined as a dicentric, a centric ring, or a deletion exceeding 3 Mbp (McMahon and Prise 2021). To account for dose-rate effects, time stamps were assigned to individual SPT-SDD datasets based on the experimental dose rate of 0.55 Gy min−1 (Cornforth et al 1987). This predictive modeling was performed for all 10 000 cells in the batch to generate the final population-level dose-response curves.

        2.3.2. Simulation of cells irradiated with protons at the distal edge of a clinical proton beam

        In Step 1, we used TOPAS to simulate a multi-energy (70–150 MeV) clinical proton beam based on parameters provided by the Emory Proton Therapy Center. As illustrated in figure 4, the simulation geometry consisted of a circular proton pencil beam incident perpendicularly on a cuboidal water phantom. Proton energy spectra were tallied at the entrance and at various depths near the distal edge of the spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP). This distal region is of particular interest because protons there are of low energies (<10 MeV), which correspond to intermediate LET and a resultant increase in RBE (Matsumoto et al 2014, Marshall et al 2016). To ensure statistical accuracy, a total of 108 proton histories were simulated.

        The proton spectra obtained from the TOPAS simulations are displayed in figure 5, comparing the beam entrance (0 cm depth) with the distal edge of the SOBP (16 cm depth). At the entrance, the spectrum reflects the typical incident beam energies of 70–150 MeV. In contrast, the distal edge spectrum exhibits a significant low-energy component (<10 MeV), representing intermediate-LET protons (∼5 keV µm−1) entering the cell nucleus. This shift is critical as it marks the region where LET and RBE rise sharply. We note that despite differences in incident beam spectra between our model and those used in previous studies (Matsumoto et al 2014, Marshall et al 2016), the proton spectral characteristics at the distal edge are inherently similar across different therapeutic proton energies.

        In Step 2, we converted the distal-edge proton spectrum into a CDF to retrieve and assemble SPT-SDD library datasets into dose-specific composite files, which were subsequently used in Step 3 to evaluate biological outcomes on cell-by-cell basis.

        2.3.3. Simulation of in-vitro cells irradiated with alpha particles

        In Step 1, we used TOPAS simulate the in-vitro alpha-particle irradiation experiments described in several landmark studies (Inkret et al 1990, Eisen et al 1991, Raju et al 1991, Cornforth et al 2002). The simulation geometry, illustrated in figure 6, consists of a planar 238Pu source and a 1.5 µm Mylar window, with the cell layer positioned directly above the window. Alpha-particle energy spectrum was tallied as each alpha particle enters a cell nucleus. To ensure statistical accuracy of the energy spectrum, we simulated 107 alpha-particle histories. As shown in figure 7, our simulated spectrum aligns closely with that measured in the prior study (Eisen et al 1991), with both exhibiting a mean energy of approximately 3.5 MeV.

        3. Results

        This section presents the results for the SPT-SDD library generation (section 2.1) followed by the simulation of in vitro irradiation scenarios involving 280 kVp x-rays, clinical distal-edge protons, and alpha particles (sections 2.3.1–2.3.3). To evaluate the efficiency of our methodology, we provide a comparison of the computational time required for a single-cell simulation using our method versus traditional ‘brute-force’ TOPAS-nBio simulations. Furthermore, our results are benchmarked against previously reported in vitro measurements, with a detailed analysis of the resulting agreements and discrepancies provided in Section 4.0.

        3.1. DSBs produced by electrons, protons, and alpha particles using TOPAS-nBio

        Figure 9 shows the simulated results of DSB yield (DSB cell−1 Gy−1) as a function of kinetic energy for electrons, protons, and alpha particles, respectively, calculated using TOPAS-nBio version 4.0. Additionally, a complementary plot illustrating the DSB yield as a function of LET for these particles is provided in supplementary figure S.5. The data points shown in the figure are the direct outputs of TOPAS-nBio and are included in the pre-calculated SPT-SDD data library. Each data point corresponds to a single energy bin in the library and was obtained from an ensemble of SPT-SDD datasets containing more than 10 000 total DSBs. The statistical uncertainty associated with each data point is approximately 1%.

        All three particle types exhibit a non-monotonic relationship between energy and DSB yield, characterized by a distinct maximum. For high-energy particles, yields plateau at 34, 43, and 65 DSBs cell−1 Gy−1 for electrons (>250 keV), protons (>10 MeV), and alpha particles (at 8 MeV), respectively. As energy decreases and LET increases, these yields rise to their respective maxima: 45.3 DSBs cell−1 Gy−1at 5 keV for electrons, 88.2 DSBs cell−1 Gy−1 at 250 keV for protons, and 95.3 DSBs cell−1 Gy−1 at 1.5 MeV for alpha particles. In all cases, a subsequent decrease in yield is observed at the lowest energy levels.

        Overall, figure 9 illustrates a distinct energy dependence in DSB yields across all three particle types, driven by changes in track structure and localized energy deposition density as particle kinetic energy changes. Since the accuracy of our simulation framework is fundamentally dependent on the pre-calculated SPT-SDD data library, the rigorous validation of these yields is essential to ensure the reliability of the entire model.

        4. Discussion

        Validation of the SPT-SDD library is supported by the DSB yields presented in figure 9, which reproduce the expected energy-dependent trends for electrons, protons, and alpha particles. While prior studies have highlighted the sensitivity of DSB predictions to physics models and DNA geometry (Zhu et al 2020b; Bertolet et al 2022), our simulations—leveraging the refined geometry and scoring of TOPAS-nBio 4.0—demonstrate strong agreement with experimental benchmarks (Ward 1988, Frankenberg et al 1999, Höglund et al 2000). This quantitative consistency implies that any observed discrepancies in downstream biological endpoints are likely independent of initial DSB quantification. Furthermore, although figure 9 plots these yields against particle energy, the corresponding LET dependence is inherently preserved through the monotonic relationship between energy and LET in water. These results align with previous TOPAS-nBio-based research, confirming the reliability and reproducibility of our library.

        The accuracy of propagating initial DNA damage into biological endpoints using MEDRAS-MC varies with radiation quality. For low-LET radiation (280 kVp x-rays), the simulated cell survival curves show strong agreement with in vitro data at low doses (<4 Gy). However, a noticeable discrepancy emerges at higher dose levels, where the simulation predicts lower survival fractions than those observed experimentally (figure 10). This deviation likely stems from the use of a fixed σ value (194 nm) in the MEDRAS-MC calculations across the entire dose range. In this model, σ represents the characteristic rejoining distance between two DNA break ends. As dose increases, the higher density of DSBs leads to more decondensed chromosome regions, which should theoretically increase the effective rejoining range. Therefore, while a constant σ value was used here, these findings suggest that future MEDRAS-MC simulations for low-LET scenarios should incorporate dose-dependent σ values to improve accuracy.

        For intermediate and high LET radiations, figures 11 and 12 show that our simulations systematically overestimate cell survival as LET increases. These discrepancies suggest that the current MEDRAS-MC repair model does not fully account for the lethal potential of clustered and complex DSBs. As documented by Rydberg (1996) and Löbrich et al (1996), these DSB clusters frequently generate short, kbp-sized DNA fragments. Such fragments, which are characteristic of high-LET radiation, may trigger apoptotic pathways as suggested by Chauhan et al (2011) and Yamakawa et al (2008). We verified the prevalence of these clusters in our simulation, finding that 3.5 MeV alpha particles produce DSBs with a median proximity of 75 nm (figure S.1.2). While the proximity-based rejoining function by McMahon and Prise is incorporated into MEDRAS-MC, it likely requires refinement to better capture the ‘repair-resistant’ nature of these localized clusters. Without a specialized treatment for these complex lesions, the model inherently biases toward successful repair, leading to the observed overestimations of cell survival for high-LET radiation.

        Beyond replicating experimental trends, our in-silico approach offers significantly greater statistical accuracy than in-vitro methods, especially regarding chromosome aberration yields. This underscores the utility of the simulation for characterizing rare biological events that are often difficult to quantify experimentally.

        The primary advantage of our methodology—its exceptional computational efficiency—is detailed in section 3.5. It shows that brute-force MCTS simulations, particularly for high-LET particles, are computationally prohibitive and often impractical on personal computing systems. The pre-calculated SPT-SDD library enables a dramatic reduction in computation time, achieving speedups of up to three orders of magnitude. This improvement makes high-fidelity, cell-by-cell radiobiology simulations feasible on widely accessible computing platforms.

        To our knowledge, this work introduces the first end-to-end MC framework—linking TOPAS-nBio track-structure simulations with MEDRAS-MC—capable of predicting chromosome aberrations and cell lethality on a cell-by-cell basis. This stochastic approach naturally accounts for both physical and biological variability, with results reflecting inherent statistical uncertainties. While the approach reliably captures low-LET responses and qualitative trends across radiation qualities, further developments in MEDRAS-MC are required to accurately model high-LET lethality. Incorporating special treatment of complex DSBs and clustered DSBs will be essential for achieving quantitative agreement with experimental observations across diverse irradiation conditions.
