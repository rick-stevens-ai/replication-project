# Onset of random matrix behavior in scrambling systems

E-mail: [hrant@stanford.edu](mailto:hrant@stanford.edu), [hanada@yukawa.kyoto-u.ac.jp](mailto:hanada@yukawa.kyoto-u.ac.jp), [sshenker@stanford.edu](mailto:sshenker@stanford.edu), [tezuka@scphys.kyoto-u.ac.jp](mailto:tezuka@scphys.kyoto-u.ac.jp)

Abstract: The fine grained energy spectrum of quantum chaotic systems is widely believed to be described by random matrix statistics. A basic scale in such a system is the energy range over which this behavior persists. We define the corresponding time scale by the time at which the linearly growing ramp region in the spectral form factor begins. We call this time tramp. The purpose of this paper is to study this scale in many-body quantum systems that display strong chaos, sometimes called scrambling systems. We focus on randomly coupled qubit systems, both local and k-local (all-to-all interactions) and the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model. Using numerical results, analytic estimates for random quantum circuits, and a heuristic analysis of Hamiltonian systems we find the following results. For geometrically local systems with a conservation law we find tramp is determined by the diffusion time across the system, order N<sup>2</sup> for a 1D chain of N qubits. This is analogous to the behavior found for local one-body chaotic systems. For a k-local system like SYK the time is order log N but with a different prefactor and a different mechanism than the scrambling time. In the absence of any conservation laws, as in a generic random quantum circuit, we find tramp ∼ log N, independent of connectivity.

Keywords: AdS-CFT Correspondence, Field Theories in Lower Dimensions, Quantum Dissipative Systems, Random Systems

# <span id="page-2-0"></span>1 Introduction and summary

Dynamical aspects of many-body quantum chaos are of fundamental interest in various fields of physics, including condensed matter physics, quantum information theory and quantum gravity. Random Matrix Theory (RMT) provides us with a simple characterization of quantum chaos: if the level statistics at small energy separation agrees with RMT, one concludes the system under consideration is chaotic. There is a rich theory of this for few-body semiclassical quantum systems (for a general reference see [\[1\]](#page-57-0)). For many-body systems there is a substantial amount of numerical evidence, but not yet a fully developed theory (for recent analytic approaches see [\[2](#page-57-1)[–4\]](#page-57-2)).

This work was motivated by recent numerical work we and others [\[6–](#page-58-0)[8\]](#page-58-1) performed on the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model [\[9,](#page-58-2) [10,](#page-58-3) [12\]](#page-58-4), which has become a reference model for the study of many-body quantum chaos.

Another diagnostic of strong chaos is sensitive dependence to initial conditions, classically diagnosed by the presence of a positive Lyapunov exponent. The semiclassical quantum analog of this in few-body chaos was discussed in the 1960s [\[13\]](#page-58-5), and diagnosed by outof-time-order correlation functions (OTOC). Motivated by a desire to understand the quantum chaotic nature of black holes [\[14,](#page-58-6) [15\]](#page-58-7), these diagnostics were rediscovered in the general many-body context [\[16\]](#page-58-8) and have been studied extensively in recent years [\[10,](#page-58-3) [17](#page-58-9)[–23\]](#page-58-10). A positive value of the analogous "chaos exponent" is a signature of strong many-body chaos. Systems with such behavior are sometimes referred to as "fast scramblers" [\[15\]](#page-58-7). There is a characteristic time scale for such systems, the scrambling time tscr, that measures when an initial perturbation affecting only a few degrees of freedom has chaotically spread to all the degrees of freedom in the system.

Such systems also display a simpler signature of thermalization. Two point functions of simple operators exponentially decay. We call the decay time of such quantities the "dissipation time" tdiss. In general this time is different from the inverse of the chaos exponent.

There is also a natural time scale associated to RMT level statistics. In the energy domain one can ask how far in energy space do random matrix statistics persist, that is how many energy levels apart can one go before the characteristic correlations of RMT are lost. The inverse of this energy defines a time, which in the many-body context we will call the "ramp time", tramp for reasons that will be made clear below.

The RMT behavior of the fine-grained energy spectrum is universal in quantum chaotic systems.[1](#page-2-1) The structure of the spectrum at time scales shorter than tramp contains nonuniversal information about the specific system.

In simpler, one-body, systems this time scale has been studied extensively [\[24,](#page-59-0) [25\]](#page-59-1). For a single quantum particle propagating in a random medium, or equivalently for a banded random matrix, this time scale is the time for a particle to diffuse across the system tdiff. The corresponding energy is called the "Thouless energy" and we will sometimes refer to this time scale as the "Thouless time".

<span id="page-2-1"></span><sup>1</sup>The type of random matrix ensemble just depends on the symmetry class of the Hamiltonian.

The goal of this paper is to study the relationship between these various time scales in strongly chaotic many-body quantum systems.[2](#page-3-1) These time scales are connected, via gauge/gravity duality, to phenomena in quantum black holes. This connection provides an important motivation for our work.

To disentangle the various phenomena involved we study two different types of system: k-local systems, with degrees of freedom nonlocally coupled, but only k degrees of freedom interacting at a time; and geometrically local systems, with only nearest neighbor couplings. The k-local systems we study are the SYK model and a qubit model of pairs of qubits interacting with Gaussian random couplings which we call the "2-local Randomly Coupled Qubits (RCQ)" model. The geometrically local systems we study are a one dimensional nearest neighbor qubit model with Gaussian random couplings (the "Local RCQ") and, for reference, banded matrices.

Robust analytic techniques to study RMT phenomena in such many-body systems have not yet been developed. This is a major goal for future research. So of necessity one part of our work involves numerical investigation. But powerful techniques have been developed to study related systems, ones in which the random couplings vary at each time step. For discrete time steps these are referred to as random quantum circuits, in the continuous time limit these are called Brownian circuits.[3](#page-3-2)

As we will discuss various indicators of chaos are well captured by such systems. But a major difference with time independent Hamiltonian systems is the absence of conservation of energy. The fluctuating couplings pump energy into the system. To study the effects of conservation laws we use a recently introduced random quantum circuit that conserves spin [\[43,](#page-60-0) [48\]](#page-60-1).

We are not able to give a definitive analysis of tramp in time independent Hamiltonian systems, but are able to give a sketch of an argument using a construction discussed in refs. [\[49,](#page-60-2) [50\]](#page-60-3) that predicts results consistent with those obtained from random quantum circuits.

# Other relevant work

There has been other recent work aimed at computing tramp in the SYK model [\[3,](#page-57-3) [7,](#page-58-13) [26\]](#page-59-2). Garc´ıa-Garc´ıa and Verbaarschot give a numerical study of the number variance. They find that spectral rigidity extends over of order N<sup>2</sup> eigenvalues in the spectrum. Translating their result to the time domain they predict tramp ∼ 2 N/2/N5/<sup>2</sup> [\[7,](#page-58-13) [26\]](#page-59-2). This differs dramatically from our results. We discuss a possible explanation for this discrepancy in section [3.1.](#page-7-1)

Altland and Bagrets [\[3\]](#page-57-3) use interesting techniques from few-body quantum chaos to argue that in the SYK model tramp ∼ √ N log N. This disagrees with our N scaling. In addition the N = 34 numerical value for tramp they calculate disagrees with our numerical results presented below by roughly an order of magnitude. We do not understand this discrepancy, but make a few remarks about it in section [7.](#page-44-0) Their techniques may well serve to demonstrate the ramp at later times.

As this paper was in the final stages of editing the paper appeared. These authors [\[4\]](#page-57-2) introduce a Floquet model without local conservation law with large site dimension q that can be treated analytically in the large q limit. This allows them to compute all moments of their unitary (which in this case corresponds to time evolution) analytically, a significant advance. For a one dimensional chain they find a ramp time of log N consistent with our results. For higher dimensions they find tramp ∼ 1. For our systems we would expect a higher dimensional model with N sites to again have tramp ∼ log N. Perhaps the difference is due to the large q limit.

After submitting the first version of this paper we learned of a very interesting paper by Kos, Ljubotina, and Prosen [\[5\]](#page-58-14) that we had missed. In this paper the authors discuss a clever model that injects just enough randomness to compute all moments of their Floquet unitary even with fixed site dimension. They see the ramp as the sum of cyclically permuted ladders that give the result in RMT (as do the authors of [\[4\]](#page-57-2)) and make the connection to periodic orbit theory. They find a ramp time of log N in a way that seems quite generic, in accordance with our results. This technique looks quite powerful and seems amenable to generalization.

### <span id="page-5-0"></span>2 Random matrix theory

In this section we review a few basic properties of Random Matrix Theory (RMT) which are relevant for the following sections. For simplicity we restrict ourselves to Gaussian random Hermitian matrices, the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) [\[51\]](#page-60-6). A standard reference is [\[52\]](#page-60-7). The partition function for the GUE is given by

$$\mathcal{Z}_{\text{GUE}} = \int \left( \prod_{i,j} dM_{ij} \right) e^{-\frac{L}{2} \text{Tr} M^2}, \tag{2.1}$$

where M is an L × L Hermitian matrix. We denote its eigenvalues E<sup>i</sup> , because M is often taken to be the Hamiltonian of a quantum system. We introduce the normalized eigenvalue density

<span id="page-6-1"></span>
$$\hat{\rho}(E) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i}^{L} \delta(E - E_i). \qquad (2.2)$$

At large L the average density in this ensemble is given by the Wigner semicircle law

$$\langle \hat{\rho}(E) \rangle_{\text{GUE}} = \rho_{\text{sc}}(E) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{4 - E^2}.$$
 (2.3)

We see that with these normalizations the width of the entire band is order one and so the typical spacing between nearest neighbor eigenvalues is ∼ 1/L.

The pair correlation function R(E1, E2) = hρ(E1)ρ(E2)i is the probability of finding two eigenvalues at energies E<sup>1</sup> and E2. If their separation |E<sup>1</sup> − E2| 1 and their average E¯ = (E<sup>1</sup> + E2)/2 is not near the edge of the band R(E1, E2) is given by the sine kernel formula [\[52\]](#page-60-7), most simply stated by introducing a scaled variable

<span id="page-6-0"></span>
$$x = \pi L(E_1 - E_2)\rho(\bar{E}). \tag{2.4}$$

Then we have

$$R(E_1, E_2) = \frac{\rho_{\rm sc}(E_1)}{\pi L} \delta(E_1 - E_2) + \rho_{\rm sc}(E_1) \rho_{\rm sc}(E_2) \left(1 - \frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\right). \tag{2.5}$$

A sketch of this formula is given in figure [1.](#page-7-2) Note that 1/(Lρsc(E¯)) is the spacing between nearest neighbor energy levels in the vicinity of E¯. In regions of small ρsc the eigenvalues are farther apart. The scale of the oscillations in [\(2.5\)](#page-6-0) are set by this spacing. RMT universality means that formula [\(2.5\)](#page-6-0) holds after scaling by the local eigenvalue density. When comparing different parts of the spectrum one must scale different regions differently. This process is called "unfolding", and becomes problematic when the fluctuations in the average density are large. We will see some effects of this later.

Note that when |E<sup>1</sup> −E2| 1/L, the nonconstant part of the pair correlation behaves like <sup>1</sup> <sup>x</sup><sup>2</sup> . This shows that long range fluctuations in the eigenvalue "gas" are suppressed, a phenomenon referred to as "spectral rigidity". We can now give a more precise definition of the Thouless energy: it is the energy difference where the RMT form of spectral rigidity ceases to be valid.

In the following sections we will often use ensembles of unitary matrices. The Lie group of L×L unitary matrices has a unique measure that is invariant under left and right multiplication, Haar measure. In random matrix theory, the unitary group U(L) with Haar probability measure is called the Circular Unitary Ensemble (CUE). The eigenvalues of each matrix are of the form λ<sup>k</sup> = e iθ<sup>k</sup> , where the eigenphases are defined in the range 0 ≤ θ<sup>k</sup> ≤ 2π, and the probability density is [\[52\]](#page-60-7)

$$\rho(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_L) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\text{CUE}}} \prod_{1 \le k < j \le L} |e^{i\theta_k} - e^{i\theta_j}|^2, \qquad (2.6)$$

![](_page_7_Figure_1.jpeg)

<span id="page-7-2"></span>**Figure 1.** Pair correlation function  $R(E_1, E_2)$  plotted when  $E_2 = 0$  and  $\Delta E = E_1$  is varied. For simplicity, we have used L = 100 and have suppressed the delta function in equation (2.5). The oscillations here are at the scale of the nearest neighbor eigenvalue spacing.

where  $\mathcal{Z}_{\text{CUE}} = L! \cdot (2\pi)^L$  denotes the partition function. Note that the single eigenvalue density  $\rho(\theta)$  is uniform on the unit circle. There are no edges, unlike the semicircle. Again, we see that with these normalizations the width of the entire band is order one and so the typical spacing between nearest neighbor eigenphases  $(\theta_k) \sim 1/L$ .

The pair correlation function  $R(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \langle \rho(\theta_1) \rho(\theta_2) \rangle$  of eigenvalues is given by the sine kernel formula [52],

<span id="page-7-3"></span>
$$R(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi L} \delta(\theta_1 - \theta_2) + \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2} \left( 1 - \frac{\sin^2 L(\theta_1 - \theta_2)/2}{L^2 \sin^2((\theta_1 - \theta_2)/2)} \right). \tag{2.7}$$

Note that, unlike the GUE ensemble, this formula is exact for all values of eigenvalue differences and not just for a small window. When the eigenvalues are close, expanding the denominator yields the same answer as GUE, as expected, because the same logarithmic repulsion acts in both cases.

#### <span id="page-7-0"></span>3 Diagnostics of spectral rigidity

#### <span id="page-7-1"></span>3.1 Number variance

A standard diagnostic of spectral rigidity in random matrix theory and quantum chaos is the number variance. This was first applied to the SYK model in [7]. The number variance is defined as

$$\Sigma^{2}(K) = \langle n^{2}(E, K) \rangle - \langle n(E, K) \rangle^{2}$$
(3.1)

where n(E, K) denotes the count of levels in the interval  $[E, E+K\bar{\Delta}]$  where  $\bar{\Delta}$  is the average nearest neighbor level spacing and the computation is done after unfolding the spectrum. If RMT universality is valid we expect this quantity to be essentially independent of E.

By the definition of  $\bar{\Delta}$  we have  $\langle n(E,K)\rangle = K$  and in an interval of length K the range of the expected number of energy levels is of order

$$K \pm \sqrt{\Sigma^2(K)} \,. \tag{3.2}$$

The number variance can be expressed in terms of the pair correlation function (see for example [53]). The result for GUE is

$$\Sigma^{2}(K) = \frac{1}{\pi^{2}} \left( \log(2\pi K) + \gamma + 1 \right) + O\left(\frac{1}{L^{2}}\right), \tag{3.3}$$

where  $\gamma=0.57721\ldots$  is Euler's constant. The crucial thing here is the very slow  $\log K$  behavior which indicates a very rigid spectrum. Poisson distributed eigenvalues would give  $\Sigma^2(K) \sim K$ . This quantity has proved a very useful numerical diagnostic in various studies of few-body quantum chaotic systems [53]. But in many-body quantum chaotic systems, like SYK, there is an additional contribution to  $\Sigma^2(K)$  that obscures the RMT signal. This complication was first pointed out in the study of embedded Gaussian ensembles [54]. The basic issue is that the fluctuations in the coarse grained eigenvalue density in these systems are only suppressed by powers of 1/N. In contrast these fluctuations in random matrix theory and few-body chaos are suppressed by powers of 1/L. Here L is the dimension of the Hilbert space and in many-body systems  $L \sim e^N$ . So the density fluctuations are much larger in many-body systems.

The main effect of this on  $\Sigma^2(K)$  is easy to understand. Assume that the result of each sample is described by random matrix statistics rescaled by the eigenvalue density realized in that sample, *not* the mean eigenvalue density. Because the typical eigenvalue spacing  $\Delta$  in each sample depends on this density it varies a lot from sample to sample. This means that  $\Sigma^2(K)$  is actually of the following form (see formula 1 in [54])

$$\Sigma^{2}(K) \sim K^{2} \frac{\langle (\Delta - \bar{\Delta})^{2} \rangle}{\bar{\Delta}^{2}} + \mathcal{O}(\log K) + \dots$$
 (3.4)

For a system with zero mean energy ( $\langle \text{Tr} H \rangle = 0$ ) and small variance a rough estimate is given by<sup>5</sup>

<span id="page-8-2"></span><span id="page-8-1"></span>
$$\frac{\langle (\Delta - \bar{\Delta})^2 \rangle}{\bar{\Delta}^2} \sim \frac{\langle (\text{Tr}H^2)^2 \rangle - (\langle \text{Tr}H^2 \rangle)^2}{(\langle \text{Tr}H^2 \rangle)^2}$$
(3.5)

For GUE (3.5) is of order  $1/L^2$  so the correction in (3.4) is of order  $K^2/L^2$  which is small until K covers a finite fraction of the whole energy band. In contrast in SYK (q=4) the correction is of order  $K^2/N^4$  (see for example [8] appendix F), which becomes large at  $K \sim N^2$ . This is the same answer found by [55] and according to [26] is consistent with numerics. In SYK the spectrum consists of  $2^{N/2}$  eigenvalues and so this result would correspond to an exponentially small fraction of the spectrum. Note that this  $K^2/N^4$  behavior has nothing to do with spectral rigidity and in fact will swamp the logarithmic signal for larger energy scales. We will argue later that spectral rigidity in this system extends over  $2^{N/2}/(\sqrt{N}\log N)$  eigenvalues, corresponding to a Thouless time of  $t \sim \log N$ .

<span id="page-8-0"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>5</sup>This does not account for more complicated fluctuations in the shape of the energy spectrum.

We have made a preliminary check that this artifact is responsible for the results in [7, 26] by rescaling each sample to have the same variance. This increases the range of logarithmic behavior by roughly a factor of 2 for the largest N studied. These results are discussed in detail in appendix A.1. It is not clear to us whether there is even an in principle way to fully correct for these large fluctuations in this observable.<sup>6</sup>

#### <span id="page-9-0"></span>3.2 Spectral form factor

We now discuss another diagnostic that is less sensitive to the kind of errors discussed above, the spectral form factor. This diagnostic also has a long history in random matrix theory and quantum chaos; see e.g. [52].<sup>7</sup> It was first used to study the SYK model in [8], motivated by a finite temperature extension discussed in the context of black hole physics in [59]. Here we only consider problems with finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and focus on generic behavior in the band so we restrict ourselves to infinite temperature.

The spectral form factor is defined as follows:

$$|Z(t)|^2 = \left| \text{Tr}e^{-i\hat{H}t} \right|^2 = \left| \sum_j e^{-itE_j} \right|^2 = \sum_{ij} e^{i(E_i - E_j)t}$$
 (3.6)

Using the eigenvalue correlator we can write

$$\langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle = \int dE_1 dE_2 L^2 R(E_1, E_2) e^{i(E_1 - E_2)t}.$$
 (3.7)

We will often discuss the normalized spectral form factor

<span id="page-9-5"></span><span id="page-9-4"></span>
$$g(t) = \langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle / L^2 \tag{3.8}$$

defined so that g(0) = 1. Here again L is the dimension of the Hilbert space.

To get oriented let us look at this quantity for the GUE ensemble. As a first approximation we can replace  $\rho_{\rm sc}(E)$  in (2.3) by 1, so the eigenvalue spacing is uniform and of order 1/L all across the band.<sup>8</sup> Then we can compute the Fourier transform of (2.5) giving the result

$$\langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle \sim \begin{cases} t/(2\pi), & t < 2L \\ L/\pi, & t \ge 2L \end{cases}$$
 (3.9)

This is plotted in the right panel of figure 2 for two different energy slices with different values of  $\rho$ . We called the linear t behavior at early time the ramp. It is the time domain reflection of spectral rigidity. The Thouless energy determines the earliest time for which the linear ramp behavior holds — the ramp time,  $t_{\rm ramp}$ . In GUE this time is order one, set by the width of the band.

<span id="page-9-1"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>6</sup>Note that the variance in (3.5) goes like  $1/N^q$  for general q SYK. So studying large q will improve this statistic. But we must take  $q^2 < N$  to preserve k-locality (see [8, 56]). This is not sufficient to make the error in (3.4) smaller than the desired signal.

We call the constant region the "plateau" and the time at which it begins the "plateau time", which is of order L.

We now discuss the consequence of having an eigenvalue density  $\rho(\bar{E})$  that varies across the band. We divide the entire energy band up into a large number of regions of equal energy width narrow enough so that  $\rho$  is roughly constant in each. Changing the constant value  $\rho(\bar{E})$  in (2.5) does not change the overall form of (3.9). It only changes the plateau time and the height of the plateau (see figure 2). This means that summing up the contributions from each region to compute the total spectral form factor is very simple. Along the ramp one just adds up linear functions with equal coefficients. The final plateau height is just L, the total number of eigenvalues. The only significant change is a rounding of the sharp transition to the plateau, because different regions of the spectrum have different plateau times. The size of this effect depends on the shape of  $\rho(E)$  and depends on the system being studied.

This insensitivity of the ramp means that fluctuations from sample to sample of the eigenvalue density do not affect the ramp structure, making it a robust diagnostic of the Thouless time even without unfolding. This is in contrast to the number variance, discussed above.

There is a problem, though, with using the spectral form factor to diagnose spectral rigidity when it is very long ranged because the ramp time will be very short and the height of the ramp will be very small compared to the plateau. So small effects can hide the ramp. The GUE ensemble offers a simple example of this effect when the variation of the spectral density is included. The leading contribution at relatively early times to the spectral form factor is just given by the Fourier transform of  $\rho_{\rm sc}(E)$ ,

$$\langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle \sim L^2 \left| \int dE \rho_{\rm sc}(E) e^{-iEt} \right|^2 = L^2 \left( \frac{J_1(2t)}{t} \right)^2 \sim L^2 \frac{\cos^2(2t)}{t^3}$$
 (3.10)

<span id="page-10-1"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>9</sup>Note that from the definition (3.6) the presence of a plateau of height exactly L at sufficiently large t is manifest. The factor of  $1/\pi$  here is an artifact of the approximations involving  $\rho$ .

The  $\frac{L^2}{t^3}$  behavior for  $t \gg 1$  is due to the sharp square root edge of the semicircle distribution and dominates until the "dip time"  $t_{\rm dip}$  when it becomes smaller than the ramp, i.e., at a time when  $L^2/t_{\rm dip}^3 \sim t_{\rm dip}$ ,  $t_{\rm dip} \sim L^{1/2}$ . This early time "slope" obscures the ramp until after  $t_{\rm dip}$ , but the ramp time is typically much earlier than this. The necessity of following the ramp underneath the slope in SYK was already emphasized in [8].

There are strategies to deal with this. In GUE one can just subtract off the disconnected part of the spectral form factor, computing

$$\langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle_c = \langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle - |\langle Z(t) \rangle|^2. \tag{3.11}$$

But this does not work in the SYK model, as explained in [8]. This model has a square root edge in its eigenvalue density [8, 61, 62] but fluctuations in the edge position from sample to sample are only suppressed by a power of N, the number of fermions, rather than L (equal to  $2^{N/2}$  in SYK) as is the case in GUE. This fluctuation converts the power law slope into a Gaussian falloff in  $|\langle Z(t)\rangle|^2$ . But fluctuations in the edge position cancel out in  $\langle |Z(t)|^2 \rangle$ , leaving the  $1/t^3$  slope.

In this paper we employ the alternate strategy, suggested to us by Douglas Stanford, of using a type of microcanonical ensemble with a Gaussian window to suppress the contribution of the edge. To see the onset of the ramp we introduce a variant of the spectral form factor

<span id="page-11-2"></span>
$$|Y(\alpha,t)|^2 = \left|\sum_i e^{-\alpha E_i^2 - itE_i}\right|^2.$$
 (3.12)

In other words, we replace the energy spectrum  $\rho(E)$  with  $e^{-\alpha E^2}\rho(E)$ . This is in the spirit of a microcanonical density of states centered around E=0 but with smooth edges to the energy window that will not create spurious signals in the time domain.<sup>11</sup> Although this changes the global shape of the energy spectrum, locally it is just a multiplication by a constant factor, and hence it does not spoil the RMT universality of the energy correlations.

When  $\alpha$  is sufficiently large, any sharp edge of the spectrum is replaced with a Gaussian. Then Y, which is essentially the Fourier transform of this 'Gaussian-filtered energy spectrum', decays as a Gaussian at early time. Hence the non-universal part can be strongly suppressed, while preserving the universal part.

We also introduce the normalized quantity  $h(\alpha,t) \equiv |Y(\alpha,t)|^2/|Y(\alpha,t=0)|^2$ . Note h(0,t)=g(t).

In the GUE case at early time

$$|Y(\alpha,t)|^2 \sim L^2 e^{-t^2/\alpha^2}$$
. (3.13)

This becomes of order one at  $t \sim \sqrt{\log L}$ . So spectral rigidity can be probed out to this scale. In fact  $t_{\rm ramp} \sim 1$  here, so we cannot fully diagnose this system using this technique.

<span id="page-11-0"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>10</sup>Many plots illustrating these phenomena are displayed in [8]. Another approach was discussed in [60], where authors use a non-Gaussian potential for RMT ensemble to change the edge.

<span id="page-11-1"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>11</sup>Of course we could choose to center this window around any energy we shoose, but to maximize the number of eigenvalues we choose to center it around E = 0. We expect the same universal features to be present as long as the center of the window is not extremely close to the edge of the spectrum.

In the many-body systems we will study we expect  $t_{\text{ramp}}$  to be large enough that, in principle at least, it can be fully revealed by this measure.

In our analysis we will also discuss the analog of the spectral form factor for the Haar random CUE ensemble. Here the relevant quantity is the (k-th moment) of the trace

$$\langle |\text{Tr}(U^k)|^2 \rangle_{\text{CUE}} = \left\langle \sum_{ij} e^{i(\theta_i - \theta_j)k} \right\rangle_{\text{CUE}} = \int d\theta_1 d\theta_2 L^2 R(\theta_1, \theta_2) e^{i(\theta_1 - \theta_2)k}$$
 (3.14)

Using the exact formula for the pair correlation function (2.7) and doing the integral we get [63, 64]

$$\langle |\text{Tr}(U^k)|^2 \rangle_{\text{CUE}} = \begin{cases} k, & k < L \\ L, & k \ge L \end{cases}$$
 (3.15)

Note that the ramp and plateau are exact for the CUE, even at finite L. There is no slope because there is no eigenvalue edge. No unfolding is necessary because the eigenvalue density is uniform.

With these preliminaries out of the way we now turn to a systematic analysis of the models. We first discuss numerical data on time independent Hamiltonian systems, beginning with k-local systems.

# <span id="page-12-0"></span>4 Time independent Hamiltonian systems

#### <span id="page-12-1"></span>4.1 k-local Hamiltonian systems

k-local systems have been studied as a good model for scrambling in black holes [14, 15]. These models thermalize very quickly, with a dissipation time of order one and a scrambling time of order  $\log N$ . Diffusive effects will also be very rapid. Here we will see numerically that  $t_{\rm ramp}$  is also short, with a weak N dependence. In section 6 we will give an analytic argument that  $t_{\rm ramp}$  also scales as  $\log N$  but the physical mechanism is different from scrambling.

#### <span id="page-12-2"></span>4.1.1 SYK model

Let us start with the SYK model [9-12]. This is a quantum mechanical model of N Majorana fermions satisfying the anticommutation relation

$$\{\hat{\psi}_a, \hat{\psi}_b\} = \delta_{ab}.\tag{4.1}$$

The dimension of the Hilbert space is  $L=2^{N/2}$ . The Hamiltonian is given by

$$H = \frac{1}{4!} \sum_{a,b,c,d=1}^{N} J_{abcd} \hat{\psi}_a \hat{\psi}_b \hat{\psi}_c \hat{\psi}_d, \tag{4.2}$$

where  $J_{abcd}$  is a Gaussian random number with a mean zero and width  $\sqrt{\frac{6}{N^3}}J$ , and totally antisymmetric with respect to a, b, c, d. The disorder average (the average with respect to  $J_{abcd}$ ) is taken after calculating observables.

For this finite value of N it is impossible to do better because an envelope that decays faster in time corresponds to a Gaussian filter that is broader in energy and extends closer to the edge of the spectrum, allowing more contamination from the sharp edge. But as N grows this effect goes away because the edges of the spectrum are at energies  $\pm cN$  (even though the standard deviation of the energy is of order  $\sqrt{N}$ ). The Gaussian filter lets in an edge signal in  $\langle |Y(\alpha,t)|^2 \rangle$  of order  $e^{-2\alpha(cN)^2+2s_0N}/t^3$  where  $s_0N$  is the zero temperature entropy. The early time ramp signal is of order one. So choosing  $\alpha$  as small as  $\frac{s_0}{c^2N}$  is sufficient to mask the slope contribution from the edge. The Fourier transform of the Gaussian envelope decays  $\sim e^{2s_\infty N - \frac{t^2}{4\alpha}}$  where  $s_\infty N$  is the infinite temperature entropy. So an  $\alpha \sim 1/N$  is small enough for the Gaussian envelope to become order one in a time of order one, allowing the study of  $t_{\text{ramp}}$  as short as order one. Thus this quantity provides a reliable definition of the Thouless time in SYK and other many-body systems with similar properties. The required N values may well be computationally prohibitive though.

We discuss our detailed algorithm for determining  $t_{\min}$  in appendix A.2. The oscillations in the data make this determination quite noisy. The values listed in table 1 in the appendix provide upper bounds for  $t_{\text{ramp}}$ . The best we can say about the N dependence is that it is weak. Power laws faster than  $N^1$  and exponentials, with order one coefficients, are disfavored. A log N behavior would certainly be consistent.

#### <span id="page-14-0"></span>4.1.2 2-local randomly coupled qubits (RCQ)

#### <span id="page-17-0"></span>4.2 Local Hamiltonian systems

We will consider two examples of one dimensional local Hamiltonian systems, a nearest neighbor coupled RCQ model and the XXZ chain with random magnetic field.

#### <span id="page-17-1"></span>4.2.1 Local randomly coupled qubits (local-RCQ)

In geometrically local systems (which we will refer to as "local", as opposed to k-local) the various interesting time scales become distinct from one another and so it is easier to diagnose the effect of different physical mechanisms. In a one dimensional local spin system we expect the dissipation time  $t_{\rm diss} \sim 1$ , the scrambling time  $t_{\rm scr} \sim N$  and the diffusion time  $t_{\rm diff} \sim N^2$ . These time scales vary more quickly with N than in the k-local case making them easier to study numerically. In this system we find that  $t_{\rm ramp} \sim N^2$  and hence conjecture that it is related to diffusion, not scrambling.

# Spectral form factor

In this section, we study  $|Z|^2$  for the XXZ model (see figure 14). Again we take W = 0.5, which is in the heart of the diffusive phase. The qualitative behavior of  $|Z|^2$  is similar to the local RCQ, however it is more sensitive to finite N effects in the edge of the spectrum.

As in the local RCQ, the intermediate region lasts longer than in the k-local spin chain. We determine  $t_{\rm ramp}$  in exactly the same way as in section 4.2.1. However, for this system the numerical analysis is more subtle. For the small values of W that are in the diffusive regime there are some approximate conservation laws that produce oscillating behavior at early time and make it difficult to determine the ramp time accurately so we do not present results.

If one increases W the system moves toward the MBL phase and a rather long intermediate region appears in the spectral form factor (see figure 15). At this point, we do not have a good theory to explain the shaded region for values of the  $W \geq 1$ . Perhaps it is related to the subdiffusive regime between ergodic and MBL phases.

These numerical results are inconclusive, but as we will see in section 5.2, the random circuit analogue of this model provides an ideal setup to test our proposal that  $t_{\text{ramp}}$  and  $t_{\text{diff}}$  are the same.

#### <span id="page-22-0"></span>5 Random/Brownian quantum circuit models

We now discuss analytic methods. In contrast to few-body systems analytic approaches for time independent Hamiltonian many-body systems have not yet been fully developed. Instead we turn to a related class of systems that display similar thermalization behavior

and are analytically tractable. These are random quantum circuit and Brownian quantum circuit models.

In Brownian circuit models, the Hamiltonians are chosen randomly from certain ensembles, independently at each small time step dt. After M steps, the unitary evolution will be given by

$$U(t) = e^{-iH_M dt} e^{-iH_{M-1} dt} \cdots e^{-iH_1 dt}$$
(5.1)

where t = Mdt and each of the operators  $H_i$  is independently drawn from a given ensemble. A random quantum circuit is a discrete version of this where each  $e^{-iH_idt}$  is replaced by a unitary drawn from a suitable ensemble of unitary "gates". If the  $H_i$  are chaotic we expect that U(t) performs a kind of random walk on the unitary group U(L), eventually converging to the Haar random ensemble. The analogous question concerning the onset of random matrix behavior becomes the question about how quickly the spectrum of U(t) starts displaying CUE statistics. A number of recent papers have studied thermalization in various correlations functions using these techniques [18, 22, 41–43].

The conclusion of all this work is that appropriate Brownian/random quantum circuits display known thermalizing behaviors — two point function decay (dissipation); chaotic/scrambling behavior as diagnosed by OTOCs and subsystem density matrices; and diffusion — with mechanisms and time scales that are very much analogous to fixed Hamiltonian systems. We will assume for now that Brownian/random quantum circuits will also display the onset of random matrix behavior via analogous mechanisms and with the same time scales as fixed Hamiltonian systems with the same locality properties and conservation laws. Because of their more efficient randomization we expect that at the least the time scales in these models are a lower bound for those in the coresponding Hamiltonian systems.

All the results we obtain are consistent with the Hamiltonian numerics discussed above. In addition these results are consistent with a heuristic framework for an analytic argument for Hamiltonian systems we discuss in section [6.](#page-40-1) In any case we find this assumption to be a very plausible one, and now turn to working out its consequences.

Because of the Brownian nature of these systems the naive analog of the spectral form factor |TrU(t)| 2 decays to the value given by the average using Haar measure, which we call the Haar value, and stays there. We call the time when this value is attained the 'Haar time' tHaar.

But this does not probe the full range of correlations in the spectrum of U(t). To do this imagine running the quantum circuit for a time t, and after that repeating the same time evolution.[18](#page-24-0) Then the time evolution from t = 0 to t˜= kt (k = 1, 2, · · ·) is described by U(t˜) = (U(t))<sup>k</sup> . The analog of the spectral form factor for time t˜ is then

<span id="page-24-4"></span>
$$u(k,t) \equiv \left\langle |\text{Tr}(U(t))^k|^2 \right\rangle. \tag{5.2}$$

Note that u(k, t) = k for k ≤ L when U(t) is drawn from the Haar (CUE) ensemble (see discussions in section [2](#page-5-0) and appendix [B.1\)](#page-48-1). This is just a discrete version of the ramp. The time t at which the initial moments approach this value, tHaar, is thus the analog of tramp in the Hamiltonian systems discussed in previous sections. Hopefully the physical meaning of tramp can be understood if we understand the meaning of tHaar.

An important question is whether tHaar depends on k. We are able to explore k = 1, 2 analytically. We then appeal to random matrix theory intuition that suggests the finer grain structure of the eigenvalue distribution is easier to establish in a chaotic system than the coarse grained structure. Since higher k probes finer structure we expect, at least for large enough k, that tHaar will, at the least, not increase as k increases. Our numerics, discussed below, are consistent with this and seem to indicate that k = 2 is already in the stable range.[19](#page-24-1)

In this section we consider Brownian circuit versions of the RCQ and XXZ models, both local and 2-local.[20](#page-24-2) In earlier sections we suggested that for geometrically local systems tramp is the diffusion time. An essential difference between the random/Brownian quantum circuit and time-independent Hamiltonian system is that the former does not conserve energy and hence in general there is no diffusion. To study the role of diffusion in this context we use a random circuit where spin, not energy, is conserved. In particular we study a random circuit version of the XXZ model introduced by [\[43,](#page-60-0) [48\]](#page-60-1) in section [5.2.](#page-33-0) [21](#page-24-3) There we will see it is possible to relate tHaar and hence tramp to the diffusion time, distinguishing it from the scrambling time. In the 2-local case we show that diffusion happens in order one time, and is not important for setting tramp.

### <span id="page-25-1"></span>5.1.1 Analytic estimate

Here we provide a calculation of the spectral form factor for random circuits. Specifically, we are interested in the behavior of u(k,t) defined by (5.2). We first describe the k=1 case, which is conceptually quite simple.

We use techniques presented in ref. [34]. We will investigate the parallelized, local random circuit model shown in the left part of figure 16, where each step consists of two layers acting on pairs of qubits and each gate is selected at random from some 2 qubit universal gate set  $\Gamma \subseteq U(4)$ . For general k we can express  $\text{Tr}U^k$  as a sum of monomials,

$$\operatorname{Tr} U^k = \sum_{i_1, i_2, \dots, i_k = 1}^L U_{i_1 i_2} U_{i_2 i_3} \dots U_{i_k i_1}, \tag{5.3}$$

where  $L=2^N$  is the dimension of the Hilbert space. Therefore, (5.2) can be written as

$$u(k,t) = \left\langle \left| \text{Tr} U^k \right|^2 \right\rangle = \sum_{i,j} \left\langle U_{i_1 i_2} U_{i_2 i_3} \dots U_{i_k i_1} U_{j_1 j_2}^* U_{j_2 j_3}^* \dots U_{j_k j_1}^* \right\rangle.$$
 (5.4)

# Calculating $\left\langle \left| \text{Tr } U(t) \right|^2 \right\rangle$

We want to study

$$u(1,t) = \left\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \right\rangle = \sum_{i,j=1}^{L} \left\langle U_{ii}U_{jj}^* \right\rangle. \tag{5.5}$$

There are two types of terms we need to study: i = j and  $i \neq j$ . Without loss of generality we can set i = 1.

First let us consider  $U_{11}U_{11}^*$ . In a natural 0 (up spin) and 1 (down spin) basis, i=1 is  $|0,0,\cdots,0\rangle = |0\rangle^{\otimes N}$ , and

$$U_{11}(t) = \langle 0, 0, \cdots, 0 | \hat{U}(t) | 0, 0, \cdots, 0 \rangle.$$
 (5.6)

By using a density matrix ˆρ(t) = Uˆ(t)ˆρ(0)Uˆ† (t) with the initial condition

$$\hat{\rho}(0) = |0, 0, \cdots, 0\rangle \langle 0, 0, \cdots, 0| = (|0\rangle \langle 0|)^{\otimes N}$$

$$(5.7)$$

at t = 0, we can write it as

$$U_{11}(t)U_{11}^{*}(t) = \text{Tr}\Big[\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{\rho}(0)\Big].$$
 (5.8)

The density matrix can be expanded in tensor products of strings of Pauli matrices σp, where p is a label specifying a Pauli string,[22](#page-26-0) as

$$\rho(t) = 2^{-N/2} \sum_{p} \gamma_t(p) \sigma_p. \tag{5.9}$$

Note that Tr(ρ(t)) = 1 holds for any t due to the unitarity of time evolution. We use p = 0 to denote the identity matrix, and so γt(0) = 2−N/<sup>2</sup> for any t. By using these expansion coefficients γt(p), we obtain

$$U_{11}U_{11}^* = 2^{-N} \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \sum_{p} \gamma_t(p) \sigma_p \times \sum_{q} \gamma_0(q) \sigma_q \right]$$
 (5.10)

$$= \sum_{p,q} \gamma_t(p) \gamma_0(q) \delta_{pq} \tag{5.11}$$

$$=2^{-N} + \sum_{p\neq 0} \gamma_t(p)\gamma_0(p). \tag{5.12}$$

We can easily determine γ0(p), by noticing that

$$\hat{\rho}(0) = (|0\rangle \langle 0|)^{\otimes N} = \left(\frac{1}{2}(I+Z)\right)^{\otimes N}, \tag{5.13}$$

where I = σ <sup>0</sup> and Z = σ 3 . Namely γ0(p) is nonzero only when σ<sup>p</sup> is a Pauli string containing only I and Z's (equivalently, when p is a set consisting only from 0 and 3), and all nonzero elements are 2−N/<sup>2</sup> . Therefore,

$$\langle U_{11}U_{11}^*\rangle = 2^{-N} + 2^{-N/2} \sum_{p'} \langle \gamma_t(p') \rangle,$$
 (5.14)

where p 0 runs through all possible sets of 0 and 3 but (0, 0, · · · , 0).

For a given choice of circuit the Pauli chain of I's and Z's evolves into other chains with positive and negative weights. On averaging these tend to cancel and hγt(p 0 )i decays exponentially.[23](#page-26-1) A pair II is left unchanged by the evolution. So the only invariant chain is all I's. The rate of decay of other chains is proportional to the number of Z's in the

<span id="page-26-0"></span><sup>22</sup>More precisely, p is a set of N numbers (p1, p2, · · · , p<sup>N</sup> ), where p<sup>i</sup> = 0, 1, 2 or 3, and σ<sup>p</sup> = σ <sup>p</sup><sup>1</sup> ⊗ σ <sup>p</sup><sup>2</sup> ⊗ · · · ⊗ σ <sup>p</sup><sup>N</sup> . Here σ 0 is the 2 × 2 identity matrix and σ 1,2,3 are Pauli matrices σ x,y,z .

<span id="page-26-1"></span><sup>23</sup>In fact for gates chosen randomly from the Haar distribution on U(4) the average hγt(p 0 )i drops to zero in one step.

chain, which we will call d. Lemma 4.1 (section 4.1) of ref. [34] bounds the one and two norms of  $\gamma_t(p)$  for the random circuit. Their bound is

$$\sum_{p'} \left\langle \left| \gamma_t(p') \right| \right\rangle \le \sum_{d=1}^N e^{-\Delta t d} \sum_{d(p')=d} |\gamma_0(p')|. \tag{5.15}$$

where d(p') is a number of Pauli-Z's in  $\sigma_{p'}$  and  $\Delta$  is the 'gap parameter' which depends on the choice of the gate set  $\Gamma$ . Thus, we can bound the sum as

$$\sum_{p'} \left\langle \left| \gamma_t(p') \right| \right\rangle \le \sum_{d=1}^N e^{-\Delta t d} \sum_{d(p')=d} |\gamma_0(p')|. \tag{5.16}$$

In our normalization, for both local and k-local RCQ,  $\Delta$  should be of order 1, independent of the system size N. In our case  $\sum_{d(p')=d} |\gamma_0(p')| = 2^{-N/2} \binom{N}{d}$ , and hence

$$\langle U_{11}U_{11}^*\rangle \le 2^{-N} + 2^{-N} \sum_{d=1}^N e^{-\Delta t d} \binom{N}{d} = 2^{-N} + \left(2^{-N} \left(1 + e^{-\Delta t}\right)^N - 1\right).$$
 (5.17)

Next we study the behavior of  $U_{11}U_{jj}^*$ . The *j*-th state is represented by a sequence of 0's and 1's. As we will see, the number of 1's, which we denote by q, controls the decay rate. Hence let us denote the *j*-th state by  $|1^q0^{N-q}\rangle$ , neglecting the ordering of 0's and 1's. We consider the time evolution of an operator  $\hat{O}(t)$ , whose initial condition is specified by

<span id="page-27-1"></span>
$$\hat{O}(0) = |1\rangle \langle j| = |0^N\rangle \langle 1^q 0^{N-q}|. \tag{5.18}$$

By definition,

$$\hat{O}(t) = \hat{U}(t)\hat{O}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t), \tag{5.19}$$

and hence

$$U_{11}U_{jj}^{*} = \left\langle 0^{N} \middle| \hat{U}(t) \middle| 0^{N} \right\rangle \left\langle 1^{q} 0^{N-q} \middle| \hat{U}^{\dagger}(t) \middle| 1^{q} 0^{N-q} \right\rangle = \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \hat{O}(t) \hat{O}^{\dagger}(0) \right]. \tag{5.20}$$

Noticing that

$$|0\rangle\langle 1| = \frac{1}{2}(\sigma^1 + i\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{2}(X + iY),$$
 (5.21)

we can write  $\hat{O}(0)$  as<sup>24</sup>

$$\hat{O}(0) = 2^{-N/2} \sum_{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_q = 1}^{2} \sum_{d=0}^{N} \sum_{i_1 \le \dots \le i_d} 2^{-N/2} (+i)^{f(\vec{\alpha})} \sigma_1^{\alpha_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \sigma_q^{\alpha_q} \otimes \sigma_{i_1}^3 \otimes \sigma_{i_2}^3 \otimes \dots \otimes \sigma_{i_d}^3 \quad (5.22)$$

where  $f(\vec{\alpha}) = \sum_{i=1}^{q} (\alpha_i - 1)$  counts the number of  $\sigma^2$ 's. By expanding  $\hat{O}$  as we have done for  $\hat{\rho}$  as

$$\hat{O}(t) = 2^{-N/2} \sum_{p} \gamma_t(p) \sigma_p, \qquad (5.23)$$

<span id="page-27-0"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>24</sup>We have shown the expression for  $|j\rangle = |1,1,\cdots,1,0,0,\cdots,0\rangle$  for simplicity. Expressions for other orderings can easily be obtained by changing the locations of  $\sigma^{1,2}$ .

we obtain  $\gamma_0(p) = 2^{-N/2}(+i)^{f(\vec{\alpha})}$ . Hence

$$\left| U_{11} U_{jj}^* \right| = \left| \sum_{p'} \gamma_t(p') \gamma_0^*(p') \right| \le \sum_{p'} \left| \gamma_t(p') \right| \cdot \left| \gamma_0(p') \right| \tag{5.24}$$

where we sum over all the Pauli strings of the form  $XYY \dots XIIZZ \dots Z$ , that have X,Y elements for the first q sites and N-q I,Z elements for the remaining sites. The coefficients of these terms will decay as

$$\langle |\gamma_t(p')| \rangle \le e^{-\Delta t(q+d(p'))} |\gamma_0(p')|$$
 (5.25)

except for q + d(p') = 0, because the 'conversion' can take place anywhere an X, Y or Z is located. (Note that this expression, and hence the following estimate, hold for all choices of the ordering of 0's and 1's in the j-th state.) Thus, for q > 0 (i.e.  $j \neq 1$ ),

$$\langle |U_{11}U_{jj}^*| \rangle \leq \sum_{p'} \langle |\gamma_0(p)|^2 \rangle e^{-\Delta t (d(p')+q)}$$

$$= 2^{-N} e^{-\Delta t q} \sum_{d=0}^{N-q} e^{-\Delta t d} {N-q \choose d}$$

$$= 2^{-N} e^{-\Delta t q} (1 + e^{-\Delta t})^{N-q}. \tag{5.26}$$

Now we can get a good bound for u(1,t),

$$\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^{2} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{2^{N}} \sum_{j=1}^{2^{N}} \langle U_{ii}U_{jj}^{*} \rangle = 2^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{2^{N}} \langle U_{11}U_{jj}^{*} \rangle$$

$$\leq 2^{N} \sum_{q=0}^{N} 2^{-N} {N \choose q} e^{-\Delta t q} (1 + e^{-\Delta t})^{N-q}$$

$$= (1 + e^{-\Delta t})^{N} \sum_{q=0}^{N} {N \choose q} \left(\frac{e^{-\Delta t}}{1 + e^{-\Delta t}}\right)^{q}$$

$$= (1 + 2e^{-\Delta t})^{N}. \tag{5.27}$$

Hence, for each fixed N, at sufficiently late time,

$$\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \rangle - 1 \lesssim 2Ne^{-\Delta t}.$$
 (5.28)

As we have mentioned above, generic gate sets have  $\Delta$  that is O(1). If this inequality is almost saturated (numerical results explained below suggest this is actually the case), then if we plot  $\frac{\langle |{\rm Tr} U(t)|^2 \rangle - 1}{N} \sim 2e^{-\Delta t}$  as a function of t for various values of N, they should lie on top of each other when N is sufficiently large. The time scale for  $\langle |{\rm Tr} U(t)|^2 \rangle - 1$  to decay as small as  $\epsilon$  is

<span id="page-28-0"></span>
$$t_{\epsilon} \simeq \frac{1}{\Delta} \log \left( \frac{2N}{\epsilon} \right) \sim \log N.$$
 (5.29)

It is clear that this estimate  $t_{\text{Haar}}$  does not depend on the connectivity of the circuit.

We should note that we can relate this estimate to the decay of two point functions. As we show in (6.2) of section 6 we can rewrite

$$\left\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \right\rangle = 1 + \sum_{j=2}^{L^2} \frac{1}{L} \left\langle \text{Tr}\left(U^{\dagger}(t)P_jU(t)P_j\right) \right\rangle$$
 (5.30)

<span id="page-29-0"></span>
$$=1+\sum_{j=2}^{L^2}\frac{1}{L}\left\langle \operatorname{Tr}\left(P_j(t)P_j\right)\right\rangle \tag{5.31}$$

where the  $P_j$  are a complete basis of Pauli operators acting on the N qubit Hilbert space. The decay of u(1,t) is thus related to the decay of various two point functions. The content of the random circuit analysis is that this sum is dominated by the decay of the  $P_j$  containing just one Pauli. This two point function decays like  $e^{-\Delta t}$  and there are N such operators. This give the result (5.29).

# Sketch of the calculation for $u(2,t) = \left\langle \left| \operatorname{Tr} \, U^2(t) \right|^2 \right\rangle$

In the above section we have estimated the convergence of the first moment u(1,t) and shown the Haar time is  $\log N$  for both local and 2-local circuits. To fully understand the approach to random matrix statistics it is important to examine the Haar time for the k-th moment u(k,t). This is related to the study of approximate k designs. The techniques used above become quite challenging for general k and have only been carefully examined for k=2. In the appendix B.2 we carry out an analysis for u(2,t) using the techniques of ref. [34] for a particularly simple gate set, U(4) random two qubit gates. For this gate set u(1,t) converges to Haar in one time step while u(2,t) converges in  $\log N$  time. Here we give a summary of the argument.

The analysis for u(2,t) boils down to a Markov process on two copies of a string of N Paulis. When the strings are unequal their weight vanishes in one time step. For a more general gate set the k=1 rate would control their decay. When the strings are equal, a different Markov process involving transitions between pairs of Paulis controls the convergence. Again the strings with very few Z's have the slowest convergence, and cause a deviation from the Haar value of 2 of the form

$$u(2,t) \sim 2 + Ne^{-\tilde{\Delta}t} \,. \tag{5.32}$$

So u(2,t) becomes  $\epsilon$  close to Haar in a time  $\frac{1}{\tilde{\Delta}}\log\frac{N}{\epsilon}$ . Again we see a  $\log N$  dependence but here  $\tilde{\Delta}$  is different from the  $\Delta$  discussed above. It is determined by the Markov chain on pairs of Paulis. We note that the gap of the full Markov chain does not control the convergence of this quantity, as explained in appendix B.2.

We can again interpret these results in terms of two point function decay. The quantity described in (5.30) vanishes in one step for U(4) gates. But we can define "two step" correlators using equation (6.2) of section 6 to rewrite

$$\left\langle |\text{Tr}U^{2}(t)|^{2}\right\rangle = 1 + \sum_{j=2}^{L^{2}} \frac{1}{L} \left\langle \text{Tr}\left((U^{\dagger}(t))^{2} P_{j} U^{2}(t) P_{j}\right)\right\rangle$$
 (5.33)

Next we consider the Brownian circuit made from the local RCQ Hamiltonian introduced in section 4.2.1. We expect the same decay pattern as in the 2-local RCQ. We have used the same number of samples as in the 2-local RCQ Brownian circuit case.

Therefore, the early-time decay to  $\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \rangle - 1 \sim O(1)$  takes place within order one time. As shown in figure 20, right panel of figure 21 and in figure 22, the late time decay appears to be consistent with  $\tilde{c}Ne^{-\Delta_k t}$  for k = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, where  $\tilde{c}$  is an order one constant. These observations are the same as the case of the 2-local Brownian circuit. Note that here  $\Delta_1$  is larger than  $\Delta_2$ . This is not surprising given the analysis above which shows they result from somewhat different mechanisms. But for k > 2,  $\Delta_k > \Delta_2$  indicating that k = 2 is already in the stable range for determining the approach to random matrix behavior.

#### <span id="page-33-0"></span>5.2 XXZ random circuit and diffusion

In this section, we will calculate u(k,t) defined in (5.2) for an XXZ type random circuit introduced in [43, 48] which has a conserved quantity and so helps clarify the relationship between  $t_{\text{Haar}}$  and  $t_{\text{diff}}$ . The quantum circuit is composed of a brickwork array of two-qubit gates as in previous examples (see figure 16). In order to motivate this circuit, let us consider a Brownian circuit made from the Hamiltonian (4.8) (which we repeat here) with time varying couplings:

$$H = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{1}{4} \vec{\sigma}_i \cdot \vec{\sigma}_{i+1} + \frac{\omega_i}{2} \sigma_i^3 \right).$$

The total spin along the z-direction,  $S_z^{\rm tot}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\sigma_i^3$ , commutes with the Hamiltonian regardless of the value of the random magnetic field, so Brownian circuit time evolution commutes with  $S_z^{\rm tot}$ , and hence the total spin is conserved. The random circuit under consideration is the discrete analog of this Brownian circuit.

Because the part of the Hamiltonian relevant for the interaction between *i*-th and *j*-th sites,  $\left(\frac{1}{4}\vec{\sigma}_i\cdot\vec{\sigma}_j+\frac{\omega_i}{2}\sigma_i^3+\frac{\omega_j}{2}\sigma_j^3\right)$ , commutes with  $\sigma_i^3+\sigma_j^3$ , the unitary gate which describe

the infinitesimal time evolution takes the following form,

$$G_{i,j} = \begin{pmatrix} U_{11}^{(1)} & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & U_{11}^{(2)} & U_{12}^{(2)} & 0\\ 0 & U_{21}^{(2)} & U_{22}^{(2)} & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & U_{11}^{(3)} \end{pmatrix},$$
(5.34)

where  $U^{(1)}$  and  $U^{(3)}$  are independent random elements of U(1) and  $U^{(2)}$  is a Haar random unitary element in U(2). The indices i and j indicates the locations of the qubits. Here  $U^{(1)}$  and  $U^{(3)}$  act on the spin +1 sector ( $|0,0\rangle$ ) and the spin -1 sector ( $|1,1\rangle$ ), respectively, and  $U^{(2)}$  acts on the spin 0 sector ( $|0,1\rangle$  and  $|1,0\rangle$ ).

Because of the symmetry we use the Pauli basis  $\{I, \sigma^+, \sigma^-, Z\}$ , where

$$I = |0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|, \qquad Z = |0\rangle\langle 0| - |1\rangle\langle 1|, \qquad (5.35)$$

$$\sigma^{+} = \frac{1}{2}(X + iY) = |0\rangle\langle 1|, \quad \sigma^{-} = \frac{1}{2}(X - iY) = |1\rangle\langle 0|.$$
 (5.36)

In this notation, the sum of the spins at the i-th and j-th sites is

$$\frac{\sigma_i^3 + \sigma_j^3}{2} = \frac{Z_i I_j + I_i Z_j}{2} \,. \tag{5.37}$$

The unitary gate can be expressed as

$$G_{i,j} = \frac{U_{11}^{(1)}}{4} (I_i + Z_i)(I_j + Z_j)$$

$$+ \frac{U_{11}^{(2)}}{4} (I_i + Z_i)(I_j - Z_j) + U_{12}^{(2)} \sigma_i^+ \sigma_j^- + U_{21}^{(2)} \sigma_i^- \sigma_j^+ + \frac{U_{22}^{(2)}}{4} (I_i - Z_i)(I_j + Z_j)$$

$$+ \frac{U_{11}^{(3)}}{4} (I_i - Z_i)(I_j - Z_j) .$$

$$(5.38)$$

We now consider the adjoint action of  $G_{i,j}$  on pairs of Paulis in the string. This has the following properties [43]: (for simplicity, we suppress i, j indices):

- 1. II, ZZ and (IZ + ZI)/2 are invariant.
- 2. (IZ ZI)/2,  $\sigma^+\sigma^-$  and  $\sigma^-\sigma^+$  will mix among each other with equal probability. After Haar averaging, these terms vanish.
- 3.  $\sigma^+\sigma^+$  and  $\sigma^-\sigma^-$  get random phases  $U_{11}^{(1)}U_{11}^{(3)*}$  and  $U_{11}^{(1)*}U_{11}^{(3)}$ , respectively. Because of these phases, the averages become zero.
- 4. The raise-by-one operators  $\sigma^+ I$ ,  $\sigma^+ Z$ ,  $I\sigma^+$  and  $Z\sigma^+$  will transition between each other with equal probability. The averages of these linear combinations are zero. The same will happen to the lower-by-one operators.

The important point here is that any chain other than II, ZZ and (IZ + ZI)/2 are completely randomized after one application of the unitary gate, so that they average

to zero immediately. The transitions between the remaining I, Z chains form a Markov process. Note that unlike the U(4) gate the evolution is a Markov chain already at k = 1. This Markov chain can be understood more easily if we use another basis

$$P = |0\rangle\langle 0| = \frac{I+Z}{2}, \qquad M = |1\rangle\langle 1| = \frac{I-Z}{2}.$$
 (5.39)

In this basis, PP and MM are invariant, while PM and MP are mapped to  $\frac{1}{2}PM + \frac{1}{2}MP$ . The Markov property is now evident.

# <span id="page-35-0"></span>5.2.1 $\left\langle \left| \text{Tr } U(t) \right|^2 \right\rangle$ in the XXZ random circuit

Let us use the setup above to estimate the k = 1 moment.

<span id="page-35-1"></span>
$$\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \rangle = \sum_{i,j=1}^{2^N} \langle U_{ii}U_{jj}^* \rangle.$$
 (5.40)

We first consider the 'local' model defined above, which has nearest-neighbor interactions via the gates  $G_{i,i,+1}$ .

As in the case of RCQ, we can write the spectral form factor as

<span id="page-35-2"></span>
$$\langle U_{ii}U_{jj}^*\rangle = \left\langle \operatorname{Tr} \left[ U_t |i\rangle\langle j|U_t^{\dagger}|j\rangle\langle i| \right] \right\rangle$$
 (5.41)

where as before  $|i\rangle\langle j|$  can be expressed as a sum over strings of  $\sigma$ -matrices. Let us use the  $M, P, \sigma^{\pm}$  basis. When  $i \neq j$ , at least one  $\sigma^{\pm}$  appears in the chain. Then, as we have seen, as soon as  $\sigma^{\pm}$  is hit by a random gate,  $U_t(\sigma\sigma\cdots)U_t^{\dagger}$  averages to zero. Hence contributions from  $i \neq j$  terms disappear in one time step. Therefore we only need to consider i = j.

First let us consider the case with a single M,

$$\langle U_{ii}U_{ii}^*\rangle = \left\langle \operatorname{Tr} \left[ U_t \left( PP \cdots PMP \cdots P \right) U_t^{\dagger} \left( PP \cdots PMP \cdots P \right) \right] \right\rangle. \tag{5.42}$$

We need to see how  $U_t(PP\cdots PMP\cdots P)U_t^{\dagger}$  evolves. After averaging MP and PM evolve to  $\frac{PM+MP}{2}$ , while PP is invariant. Hence, when the random gate hits MP, the chain remains  $MPP\cdots P$  with probability 1/2, and changes to  $PMP\cdots P$  with probability 1/2. So M performs a random walk in the string. Because there is no preferred direction there is no net drift of the walker. We analyze this system in detail below but for now make a few qualitative remarks.

The probability distribution of M locations spreads diffusively so  $\langle U_{ii}U_{ii}^*\rangle$ , given by the overlap with the initial state, decays like  $\langle U_{ii}U_{ii}^*\rangle \sim 1/\sqrt{t}$  at early time. At late time it is uniformly spread on the string and eventually  $\langle U_{ii}U_{ii}^*\rangle$  converges to 1/N. For general i there are q M's and N-q P's. Then the diffusive decay at early time is  $\left(1/\sqrt{t}\right)^q$  (when  $q \ll N$ ). The late-time value is  $1/\binom{N}{q}$ .

In order to give a precise analysis we identify M and P with two dimensional vectors,

$$|M\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\0 \end{pmatrix}$$
 and  $|P\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0\\1 \end{pmatrix}$ . (5.43)

In other words we identify |Mi and |Pi to be two states of a qubit. Let us denote by Pi,j a unitary operator that acts on the vector space of N-qubits and swaps the states in i, j positions:

$$P_{i,j}\left(|P\rangle_{i}\otimes|M\rangle_{j}\right) = |M\rangle_{i}\otimes|P\rangle_{j},$$

$$P_{i,j}\left(|M\rangle_{i}\otimes|P\rangle_{j}\right) = |P\rangle_{i}\otimes|M\rangle_{j},$$

$$P_{i,j}\left(|M\rangle_{i}\otimes|M\rangle_{j}\right) = |M\rangle_{i}\otimes|M\rangle_{j},$$

$$P_{i,j}\left(|P\rangle_{i}\otimes|P\rangle_{j}\right) = |P\rangle_{i}\otimes|P\rangle_{j}.$$
(5.44)

A single step of our process can be identified with the action of

$$\widetilde{Q}_{loc} = \left[ \bigotimes_{i \in even} \frac{1}{2} \left( P_{i,i+1} + I \right) \right] \cdot \left[ \bigotimes_{i \in odd} \frac{1}{2} \left( P_{i,i+1} + I \right) \right]. \tag{5.45}$$

Instead of analyzing this Markov process, we will approximate it by N repetitions of a process where in each step a random site i is selected and the gate is applied between i and i + 1. This should approximate the original parallelized circuit at long times and in particular we expect it to give the same N scaling of the gap.[27](#page-36-0) The Markov operator for this process is

$$Q_{\text{loc}} = \left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2} \left( P_{i,i+1} + I \right) \right]^{\otimes N} . \tag{5.46}$$

The number r of M's in a string is conserved by this process. We will use the subscript r to denote the restricted action of Qloc,<sup>r</sup> in this subspace. This process has already been studied extensively. It is related to an interchange model on cards studied by Diaconis and Ng [\[71\]](#page-61-8).[28](#page-36-1) They point out that this model can be mapped to a Heisenberg spin chain

$$Q_{\text{loc}} = \left[ \frac{3}{4} I - \frac{1}{2N} H_{XXX} \right]^{\otimes N}, \text{ where } H_{XXX} = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_i \cdot \vec{\sigma}_{i+1}.$$
 (5.47)

This connection becomes clear by noting that

$$P_{i,j} = \frac{1}{2} \left( I + \vec{\sigma}_i \cdot \vec{\sigma}_j \right) . \tag{5.48}$$

It is known that the interchange model for local and 2-local cases defines a reversible and irreducible Markov chain. This process has a uniform stationary distribution on the N r different strings. The largest eigenvalue of Qloc,<sup>r</sup> corresponding to the stationary distribution will be λ0,r = 1. and all the eigenvalues, as we will discuss, will be between 0 and 1.

<span id="page-36-0"></span><sup>27</sup>We confirm numerically in the next section that the analytic results for the Markov dynamics of Qloc are fully consistent with the numerical results on the original parallelized circuit <sup>Q</sup>eloc.

<span id="page-36-1"></span><sup>28</sup>We thank Persi Diaconis for bringing this work to our attention.

Now we go through a standard Markov chain analysis. Suppose we have an initial string with r M's, |vri = |MP PM · · · MPi. Then, we can express

$$\left\langle \operatorname{Tr}\left[U_{t}\left(MPPM\cdots MP\right)U_{t}^{\dagger}\left(MPPM\cdots MP\right)\right]\right\rangle = \left\langle v_{r}\right|Q_{\operatorname{loc}}^{t}\left|v_{r}\right\rangle$$
 (5.49)

Denote by H<sup>r</sup> the subspace of the Hilbert space spanned by vectors that have exactly r elements of M. Let us denote by λa,r the eigenvalues of Qloc,r for a = 0, 1, . . . , <sup>N</sup> r − 1 in decreasing order; 1 = <sup>λ</sup>0,r > λ1,r <sup>≥</sup> <sup>λ</sup>2,r ≥ · · · ≥ <sup>λ</sup>( N )−1,r > 0.

r The stationary state |λ0,ri is the eigenvector with the eigenvalue λ0,r = 1 and has uniform weight on each string.

Now, we can calculate u(k = 1, t) for each sector. Recalling the notation [\(5.39\)](#page-35-1) M = |1ih1| and P = |0ih0|, the number r of M's is the number of 1's in the binary representation of the state |ii appearing in [\(5.42\)](#page-35-2). In terms of the XXZ model, 0 and 1 are spin up (+1/2) and down (−1/2), so this state |ii has total spin S<sup>z</sup> = 1 2 (N −r)− 1 2 r = (N/2)−r. Therefore,

$$\left\langle |\text{Tr}U(t)|^2 \right\rangle_{S_z = N/2 - r} = \sum_{i \in \{S_z = N/2 - r\}} \left\langle U_{ii} U_{ii}^* \right\rangle = \sum_{v_r} \left\langle v_r | Q_{\text{loc},r}^t | v_r \right\rangle \tag{5.50}$$

<span id="page-37-1"></span>
$$= \text{Tr } Q_{\text{loc},r}^t = \sum_{a=0}^{\binom{N}{r}-1} \lambda_{a,r}^t$$
 (5.51)

where the |vri that are summed over are all the permutations of the vector |MP PM· · ·MPi with r M's.

We now define a gap parameter ∆<sup>r</sup> by e <sup>−</sup>∆<sup>r</sup> = λ1,r. At large N this is just the gap in the HXXX spin chain.[29](#page-37-0) We can give a simple lower bound on u(1, t) by just keeping the first two terms of [\(5.51\)](#page-37-1)

$$u(1,t) > 1 + e^{-\Delta_r t} \,. \tag{5.52}$$

So the time to get close to the Haar value[30](#page-37-2) is lower bounded by

<span id="page-37-4"></span>
$$t_{\epsilon} > \frac{1}{\Delta_r} \log \frac{1}{\epsilon} \,. \tag{5.53}$$

The gap is determined by the properties of the XXX spin chain which is one of the canonical examples of an integrable system. Reference [\[71\]](#page-61-8) quotes the following results.[31](#page-37-3) For r = 1, 2 the gap is the same and is given by ∆<sup>1</sup> = ∆<sup>2</sup> = c <sup>N</sup><sup>2</sup> where c is an order one constant. Further it is conjectured that the gap is the same for all r. Our numerics (see the following section) support this. Using this result we have

$$t_{\epsilon} > \frac{N^2}{c} \log \frac{1}{\epsilon} \,. \tag{5.54}$$

As we discussed earlier the N<sup>2</sup> dependence is a signal of diffusion.

<span id="page-37-0"></span><sup>29</sup>Up to an order one constant.

<span id="page-37-3"></span><span id="page-37-2"></span><sup>30</sup>t is a precise version of tHaar.

<sup>31</sup>We have not exhaustively studied the literature on this model. It may well be the case that more analytic results are known.

A sharp upper bound is more subtle to obtain. The terms ignored in (5.52) contribute an N dependent offset to the time required to reach the Haar value. We define  $t_{\text{offset}}$  by

$$t_{\epsilon} = t_{\text{offset}} + \frac{N^2}{c} \log \frac{1}{\epsilon} \,. \tag{5.55}$$

We can begin to estimate  $t_{\rm offset}$  by approximating the XXX chain spectrum. In the large N finite r limit the spectrum is dominated by r free magnons, each with nonrelativistic spectrum

<span id="page-38-0"></span>
$$E(p) \sim p^2/2$$
. (5.56)

Here p is a continuum momentum, where we assume  $\frac{1}{N} \ll p \ll 1$ .

Assuming  $r \ll N$  and  $t \ll N^2$  we can approximate

$$u(1,t) \approx \frac{1}{r!} \left( N \int dp e^{-p^2 t/2} \right)^r \sim \frac{1}{r!} \left( \frac{N}{\sqrt{t}} \right)^r$$
 (5.57)

This becomes order one at a time  $t_{\text{offset}} \sim \frac{N^2}{r^2}$ . Here we see that the diffusional dynamics is related to the nonrelativistic dispersion relation (5.56) of the XXX chain.

But this is not the slowest dynamics in this system. It is known that there are bound states of b magnons for all  $b \le r$ . These have "mass" of order b and hence a spectrum (see [72])

<span id="page-38-2"></span>
$$E(p) \sim p^2/(2b)$$
. (5.58)

These bound states diffuse more slowly because of their greater mass. Consider the case when all r magnons are bound up into bound states of size b, producing r/b "particles". This makes the following contribution to u(1,t):

$$u(1,t) \supset \frac{1}{(r/b)!} \left( N \int dp e^{-p^2 t/(2b)} \right)^{r/b} \sim \frac{1}{(r/b)!} \left( \frac{N}{\sqrt{t/b}} \right)^{r/b}$$
 (5.59)

This interesting function of time becomes order one at a time  $t_{\rm offset} \sim N^2 b^3/r^2$ . This suggests we can make  $t_{\rm offset} \sim N^2$  by choosing  $b \sim r^{2/3}$ . In fact it seems we can make  $t_{\rm offset}$  parametrically longer,  $\sim N^2 r$ , by choosing the largest allowed value  $b \sim r$ . For  $r \sim N$  this would give  $t_{\rm offset} \sim N^3$ . However, when  $t \sim N^2$  the discreteness of the spectrum becomes important and these approximations are no longer valid. Instead we turn to numerics (discussed below) which indicate that  $t_{\rm offset}$  is independent of r for large enough r and hence  $t_{\rm offset} \sim N^2$ .<sup>32</sup> Our best estimate becomes

$$t_{\epsilon} = \tilde{c}N^2 + \frac{N^2}{c}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}.$$
 (5.60)

Again we point out that this  $N^2$  dependence is parametrically slower than the scrambling time for this system which is N [43, 48], corresponding to ballistic propagation.

<span id="page-38-1"></span> $<sup>\</sup>overline{\phantom{a}}^{32}$ We cannot rule out a weak r dependence in  $t_{\text{offset}}$  from these numerics so for example an  $N^2 \log(N/C)$  behavior with a large C coefficient is possible.

We now turn to the 2-local XXZ random circuit. The analysis is analogous to the local system and we end up with a Markov chain. We first consider a process where at each step a gate acts on one randomly selected pair of qubits. One parallel step of the full process corresponds to N repetitions of this. The Markov operator for this process is

$$Q_{2-\text{loc}} = \left[ \frac{1}{\binom{N}{2}} \sum_{i < j}^{N} \frac{1}{2} \left( P_{i,j} + I \right) \right]^{\otimes N} . \tag{5.61}$$

The spectrum of this operator differs in important ways from the geometrically local case giving a different N scaling for  $t_{\epsilon}$ .

The spectrum of  $Q_{2\text{-loc},r}$  is related to the gap of the Heisenberg model on a complete graph. More specifically, we express

$$Q_{2\text{-loc}} = \left[\frac{3}{4}I - \frac{1}{2N}\widetilde{H}_{XXX}\right]^{\otimes N}, \quad \text{where} \quad \widetilde{H}_{XXX} = -\frac{N}{\binom{N}{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i < j}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_{i} \cdot \vec{\sigma}_{j}. \tag{5.62}$$

Then, we use a simple identity

$$\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i < j}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_i \cdot \vec{\sigma}_j = \left(\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_i\right)^2 - \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_i^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{\sigma}_i\right)^2 - \frac{3N}{4} = \vec{S}^2 - \frac{3N}{4}$$
 (5.63)

where we use  $\vec{S}^2$  to denote the total spin squared operator. So we find

$$\widetilde{H}_{XXX} = \frac{N}{\binom{N}{2}} \left( \frac{3N}{4} I - \vec{S}^2 \right) \tag{5.64}$$

Recall that  $S_z = \frac{1}{2} \sum_i^N \sigma_i^3$  and it commutes with  $\vec{S}^2$ . In the basis where both are diagonal  $\vec{S}^2$  has eigenvalues s(s+1), where  $s=0,1,\cdots,N/2$  for even N and  $S_z$  has eigenvalues  $s_z=-s,-s+1,\cdots,+s$ . Note that  $r=\frac{N}{2}-s_z$ . The eigenvalues of  $\widetilde{H}_{XXX}$  are given by

$$E_{(s,s_z)} = \frac{3N^2}{4} \frac{1}{\binom{N}{2}} - \frac{N}{\binom{N}{2}} s(s+1)$$
 (5.65)

Note that the eigenvalue is independent of  $s_z$  and only depends on s. In a given r sector, that is for a given value of  $s_z$ , all values of s such that  $\frac{N}{2} \ge s \ge s_z$  contribute.

At large N the important eigenvalues  $Q_{2\text{-loc}}$  are given by  $\lambda_a = e^{-E_a}$ . So we can write

$$u(1,t) = \sum_{a} e^{-E_a t} \tag{5.66}$$

and the gap is given by  $E_1 - E_0$ . For all r sectors  $E_0$  corresponds to  $s = \frac{N}{2}$  and  $E_1$  to  $s = \frac{N-2}{2}$ . So at large N the gap is N independent.

$$\Delta_r = E_1 - E_0 \to 2$$
. (5.67)

Diffusion on this completely connected graph takes place in order one time. The degeneracy of this first excited state is N [\[73\]](#page-61-10) and so a lower bound for u(1, t) is

$$u(1,t) > 1 + N \cdot e^{-2t} \tag{5.68}$$

giving a Haar time of

<span id="page-40-3"></span>
$$t_{\epsilon} > \frac{1}{2}\log N + \frac{1}{2}\log \epsilon \tag{5.69}$$

In fact this is an accurate estimate. The small gaps grow linearly in level, En−E<sup>0</sup> = 2n and the degeneracies grow like <sup>N</sup> n − <sup>N</sup> n−1 . [33](#page-40-2) So the full formula is well approximated by

$$u(1,t) \approx (1 + e^{-2t})^N \cdot (1 - e^{-2t}) + e^{-2t(N+1)}$$
 (5.70)

The Haar time is again given by [\(5.69\)](#page-40-3). Note again that this logarithmic dependence is due to a different mechanism than the logarithmic scrambling time.

One can study u(2, t) for this model using the techniques discussed in appendix [B.2.](#page-49-0) We do not perform a full analysis of this case, but note that the "unequal chain" part of equation [\(B.15\)](#page-51-0) (the last term) will converge slowly because of the same diffusive effects described above. This indicates that the time scale to converge to the Haar value for u(2, t) will be parametrically the same as in the u(1, t) analysis.

$$\operatorname{Tr}(\widehat{Q}) \cdot \operatorname{Tr}(\widehat{O}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L^2} \operatorname{Tr}(\widehat{Q}P_j \widehat{O}P_j)$$
(6.2)

If we select  $\widehat{Q} = \widehat{O}^{\dagger} = e^{+iHt}$  and average over the ensemble for H, we have a rewrite of the infinite temperature spectral form factor

$$\langle \operatorname{Tr}(e^{+iHt}) \cdot \operatorname{Tr}(e^{-iHt}) \rangle = 1 + \sum_{j=2}^{L^2} \frac{1}{L} \langle \operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{+iHt}P_j e^{-iHt}P_j\right) \rangle$$
(6.3)

To understand the approach to the ramp we must understand the decay of the two point correlators of all  $L^2-1$  non-identity Pauli strings  $(\langle P_j(t)P_j\rangle)$ . We intuitively expect that operators made out of more Paulis will decay faster.<sup>34</sup> This is just what the random circuit analysis shows as well (see discussion in section 5.1). So we expect the leading contribution to come from the Pauli strings with a single non-identity element — there are 3N such strings. Each of these simple two point functions will decay exponentially  $\sim e^{-mt}$  for some gap parameter m. Therefore, the spectral form factor can be approximated by

<span id="page-42-5"></span>
$$\langle \text{Tr}(e^{-iHt}) \cdot \text{Tr}(e^{+iHt}) \rangle \sim 1 + 3N \cdot e^{-mt}$$
 (6.4)

so we expect  $\log N$  scaling for the ramp time.<sup>35</sup> In a local system with diffusion we expect power law decay of correlations which would result in power law ramp times. The connection of these estimate with the random circuit calculations is clear. However, there are several issues that are significantly more subtle here.

#### **Factorization**

We assumed here that Pauli strings with d non-identity elements decay fast enough to ignore. In large N systems expect factorization

<span id="page-42-2"></span>
$$\langle (\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \dots \sigma_d)(t)(\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \dots \sigma_d)(0) \rangle$$

$$\approx \langle \sigma_1(t)\sigma_1(0)\rangle \langle \sigma_2(t)\sigma_2(0)\rangle \dots \langle \sigma_d(t)\sigma_d(0)\rangle + \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)$$
(6.5)

So we expect the early time behavior of (6.5) to be  $\sim e^{-dmt}$ . As we have seen in the random circuit analyses this exponential behavior in d is sufficiently fast to let the d=1 terms control the estimate.

But in these systems there is operator mixing at higher order in  $\frac{1}{N}$  between d Pauli operators and operators with fewer Paulis that will cause (6.5) to decay more slowly at long time. The characteristic crossover time is of order  $\log N$ ,  $^{36}$  which is the time scale of importance here. It is crucial that the operator product coefficients be sufficiently small at large N to make these slowly decaying terms subleading. This should be the case in large N systems. For instance in SYK the amplitude for 3 fermions to mix with 1 is order  $\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}$  (assuming q=4). In large N gauge theories the amplitude for 2 gluons to mix with 1 is order  $\frac{1}{N}$ . If one has a diagrammatic expansion then heuristically at least the same scalings that give a smooth large N limit ensure that no large N dependence occurs in the long time decay. Making these arguments precise and general will be crucial in turning this discussion into a sharp statement.  $^{37}$

### Edge

As we have seen a basic feature of the spectral form factor in Hamiltonian systems is the ability of the "edge" of low energy states to give a contribution that swamps the small ramp signal until exponentally late time.[38](#page-43-0) We gave a method of removing this contribution by using Gaussian envelope in the modified quantity |Y (t)| <sup>2</sup> defined in equation [\(3.12\)](#page-11-2). We need to do something similar here. It turns out the most useful quantity is a hybrid:

$$\langle Y(\alpha, t)Z(\beta, t)^* \rangle = \text{Tr}\left(e^{-\alpha H^2}e^{-iHt}\right) \cdot \text{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H}e^{-iHt}\right)$$
 (6.6)

This one sided Gaussian window is sufficient to remove the sharp edge.

To write this as a sum of correlation functions choose operators of the form <sup>Q</sup><sup>b</sup> <sup>=</sup> <sup>e</sup> −αH<sup>2</sup> e <sup>−</sup>iHt and <sup>O</sup><sup>b</sup> <sup>=</sup> <sup>e</sup> <sup>−</sup>βHe <sup>−</sup>iHt. We then have

$$\langle Y(\alpha, t)Z(\beta, t)^* \rangle = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L^2} \left\langle \text{Tr} \left( e^{-\alpha H^2} P_j(t) e^{-\beta H} P_j \right) \right\rangle. \tag{6.7}$$

In the infinite temperature limit β = 0, we will get

$$\langle Y(\alpha, t) Z(\beta = 0, t)^* \rangle = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L^2} \left\langle \text{Tr} \left( e^{-\alpha H^2} P_j(t) P_j \right) \right\rangle$$
 (6.8)

Essentially the same analysis goes through for Majorana fermions. Denote by ψ<sup>i</sup> Majorana fermions i = 1, 2, · · · , 2N. The operators live in L = 2<sup>N</sup> dimensional Hilbert space. Let us denote by

$$Q_I = \psi_{i_1} \psi_{i_2} \cdots \psi_{i_\omega} \tag{6.9}$$

where I = {i1, i2, . . . , iω}, ω is the weight of the operator and i<sup>1</sup> < i<sup>2</sup> < · · · < iω. There are total of L 2 such strings (including the identity) and we will use index I = {i1, i2, . . . , iω} → j to denote them and ω<sup>j</sup> will be the weight of the j-th element. One can check that for any operator <sup>O</sup><sup>b</sup> we can establish following identity

<span id="page-43-1"></span>
$$I \cdot \text{Tr}(\widehat{O}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L^2} 2^{\omega_j} \cdot Q_j^{\dagger} \widehat{O} Q_j$$
 (6.10)

The reason we have a dagger is that Q<sup>j</sup> can be both Hermitian and anti-Hermition. The factor 2ω<sup>j</sup> is added because ψ 2 <sup>i</sup> = I/2 for each Majorana in standard normalization. Using the same insertion as in the Pauli case we establish

$$\langle Y(\alpha, t)Z(\beta = 0, t)^* \rangle = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L^2} 2^{\omega_j} \left\langle \text{Tr} \left( e^{-\alpha H^2} Q_j^{\dagger}(t) Q_j \right) \right\rangle$$
 (6.11)

where the sum is over all the ordered Majorana fermion strings.

<span id="page-43-0"></span><sup>38</sup>This feature is absent in random circuits since energy is not conserved and a system cannot stay near the edge.

Thus, the error from the Haar value will depend on the decay of the two-point function of the simplest one fermion operators in the " $\alpha$  ensemble"

$$\langle \psi_i(t)\psi_i\rangle_{\alpha}$$
. (6.12)

In SYK this correlator can be determined by Laplace transforming the known large N finite temperature two point function. Using an  $\alpha \sim \frac{1}{N}$  which effectively removes the edge, we find results are dominated by the infinite temperature correlator which has  $m \sim 1$  (in units of J). There are N such operators so we find a decay of the form  $Ne^{-Jt}$  giving a ramp time of order  $\frac{1}{l} \log N$ , consistent with our expectations.

There is an interesting consequence of this link for the q dependence of  $t_{\rm ramp}$  in SYK.<sup>39</sup> Using the standard scaling the two point function decays like  $e^{-(\mathcal{I}t/q)}$  at large q [12, 17]. So  $t_{\rm ramp} \sim \frac{\log N}{q}$ . The chaos exponent is q independent so the scrambling time is  $\sim \log N$ , parametrically longer than the ramp time.

# <span id="page-44-0"></span>7 Closing remarks

We have argued above, both in the random quantum circuit and Hamiltonian context, that the scale for the ramp time  $t_{\text{ramp}}$  is set by the decay of N simple two point functions. The scrambling time is determined by the decay of the OTOC. Both of these quantities are driven by the underlying mechanism of operator growth under time evolution [18, 21, 74], but they diagnose different aspects of the growing operator.

This is made very clear in recent work on SYK [75].<sup>40</sup> These authors define the probability  $P_s(t)$  of having an operator of size s (i.e., containing s majorana fermions) at time t. The two point function of a single fermion is the amount of the operator remaining in that fermion  $P_1(t)$ . This decays exponentially with the dissipation time scale  $t_{\text{diss}}$ . The contribution to the spectral form factor is then  $Ne^{-t/t_{\text{diss}}}$ . This becomes order one at  $t_{\text{ramp}} \sim t_{\text{diss}} \log N$ . The (anti) commutator squared grows like the average size of the operator  $\frac{1}{N} \sum_s s P_s(t)$ . This behaves like  $\frac{e^{\lambda_{\text{L}}t}}{N}$  where  $\lambda_{\text{L}}$  is the chaos exponent, the quantum analog of the Lyapunov exponent. This becomes order one at the scrambling time  $t_{\text{scr}} \sim \frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{L}}} \log N$ . We see that these two time scales, while both displaying  $\log N$  behavior, describe quite different aspects of the growing operator. The prefactors of  $\log N$  can be parametrically different, as mentioned above. At large q,  $t_{\text{diss}} \sim \beta/q$  while  $\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{L}}} \sim \beta$ . There are many holographic examples that display this parametric separation as well.

In this paper we have studied the onset of random matrix behavior in strongly chaotic many-body systems. But it remains to give a convincing argument for random matrix behavior over the full range of energy and time scales present. For example it is an important task to give a general argument for presence of the full ramp/plateau structure in the spectral form factor. One approach that has been suggested in [2, 76] is to extend the ideas of Dyson Brownian motion used with great effect in [76] to many-body systems. Further exploring this approach is an interesting subject for future research. One important issue

<span id="page-44-1"></span> $<sup>^{39}</sup>$ Recall that q is the number of fermions coupled together in the interaction term.

<span id="page-44-2"></span><sup>&</sup>lt;sup>40</sup>We thank Alex Streicher for explaining these results to us.

will be to understand the role of the comparatively large  $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$  (rather than  $\mathcal{O}(1/L)$ ) fluctuations in eigenvalue density that are characteristic of many-body systems.

Another interesting approach has been developed recently in [3]. They apply ideas of single-body random hopping problems to the exponentially large Hilbert space of the many-body system. Their approach does not get the value of the ramp time we argue for but we think this technique may well be useful to describe the later time ramp and plateau.

This approach is closely connected to the observation in appendix F of [8] that the connected contributions to  $\langle \operatorname{Tr} H^k \operatorname{Tr} H^k \rangle$  where H is the SYK Hamiltonian have the perturbative SYK value for k fixed as N gets large, but at  $k^* \approx N \log N$  this quantity assumes the value it would have if H were a random matrix in the second quantized Hilbert space. Naively converting this to a time scale (remembering that the width of the spectrum is  $\sim \sqrt{N}$ ) one finds  $t \sim \sqrt{N} \log N$ , the scale found in [3].

The result of the calculation in [3] is a formula rather similar to (6.3), a sum over an exponentially large number of more and more rapidly decaying terms. The leading correction is of the form  $Ne^{-\mu t}$  much like (6.4). But here  $\mu \sim 1/\sqrt{N}$ , corresponding to a parametrically slower decay rate than in the SYK version of (6.4). It is this slow decay that causes the large estimate  $t_{\text{ramp}} \sim \sqrt{N} \log N$ . There are no perturbative modes in SYK with such a slow decay rate <sup>41</sup> so their significance is a puzzle to which we hope to return. <sup>42</sup>

One of the motivations for thinking about these strongly chaotic, scrambling, systems is their connection to the physics of quantum black holes. One important thing to understand is which phenomena are important in both small and large AdS black holes and which only occur in large ones. The time scale for evaporation of a small black hole is order S (where S is the black hole entropy). The ramp time we have found for the k-local systems appropriate to the black hole horizon is order  $\log S$ , parametrically smaller than the evaporation time. These effects will be exponentially subdominant in simple observables at these short times so studying them will be difficult, but they are present and are a signature of the nonperturbative quantum dynamics of these systems.

Perhaps the most important question from the gravitational point of view is the bulk dual in the sense of AdS/CFT of these random matrix phenomena [8]. In SYK a related question is the explanation of these phenomena in terms of the  $G, \Sigma$  collective fields. We have made progress on this problem which we hope to report on in the near future [70].

### <span id="page-46-1"></span>A.1 Number variance in SYK

Garc´ıa-Garc´ıa and Verbaarschot studied the number variance in the SYK model; see figure 9 and figure 10 in [\[7\]](#page-58-13). They observed a deviation from the RMT result at K ∼ N<sup>2</sup> [\[26\]](#page-59-2). In section [3.1](#page-7-1) we have explained that this very small value of K is due to the large fluctuations in the variance of the energy distribution. As a preliminary check we have implemented a slightly improved unfolding procedure:

- 1. Shift the origin of the energy spectrum so that hEi = 0 for each sample.
- 2. Then we rescale the eigenvalues for each sample so that the variance for each sample is the same as hTrH<sup>2</sup> i, the average variance.
- 3. Then we unfold each sample using the density profile determined by hρ(E)i.
- 4. Then we compute Σ<sup>2</sup> (K).

Steps (1) and (2) are not implemented in [\[7\]](#page-58-13). The number variance calculated in this manner is shown in figure [24,](#page-47-2) for N = 32 and N = 34. The agreement with RMT persists to larger K, roughly a factor of two larger, than in [\[7\]](#page-58-13). Thus we see that the number variance is sensitive to the unfolding procedure.

As discussed in the text we expect that RMT behavior extends to an exponentially large value of K, much larger than shown even by these improved results. Our unfolding procedure is still rather crude. As a first step one could try to fully unfold the spectrum sample by sample. But it is unclear to us whether this is even in principle sufficient. At a minimum an exponentially large number of samples would be required to get a sufficiently accurate value of the variance.

For this reason the number variance seems not to be the best quantity to probe long range spectral rigidity in many-body systems. As discussed in the text the spectral form factor is less sensitive to such errors.

We will use  $\tilde{\gamma}_0(p_1, p_2)$  to denote the Pauli coefficients for  $\tilde{\hat{\rho}}^{(2)}(0)$ . Recalling that

$$|0\rangle\langle 0| = \frac{1}{2}(I+Z)$$
 and  $|1\rangle\langle 1| = \frac{1}{2}(I-Z),$  (B.11)

we can see that  $\tilde{\hat{\rho}}^{(2)}(0)$  consists of the Pauli strings made only of I and Z. Therefore,

<span id="page-50-0"></span>
$$\langle U_{1j}U_{j1}U_{1j}^*U_{j1}^*\rangle = \sum_{p_1',p_2'} \gamma_t(p_1',p_2')\tilde{\gamma}_0(p_1',p_2'),$$
 (B.12)

where  $p'_1$  and  $p'_2$  run through the Pauli strings made only of I and Z. That  $\left\langle U_{1j}U_{j1}U_{1j}^*U_{j1}^*U_{j1}^*U_{j1}^*V_{j1}^*\right\rangle$  is zero at t=0 means  $\gamma_t(p'_1,p'_2)\tilde{\gamma}_0(p'_1,p'_2)$  cancel with each other at early time (due to sign differences). In order to see the time evolution of the second and third terms, we need to understand how the unitary gates selected from  $\Gamma=U(4)$  act on two copies of Pauli strings  $\sigma_{p'_1}$  and  $\sigma_{p'_2}$ . We write them as  $\sigma_{p'_1}=\sigma_{p'_{1,1}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{1,2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{p'_{1,N}}$  and  $\sigma_{p'_1}=\sigma_{p'_{2,1}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{2,2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{p'_{2,N}}$ , respectively. Because each gate acts on Pauli matrices at two sites i and j, let us focus on these two sites:  $\sigma_{p'_{1,i}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{1,j}}$  and  $\sigma_{p'_{2,i}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{2,j}}$ . The gate acts on  $(\sigma_{p'_{1,i}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{1,j}})\otimes(\sigma_{p'_{2,i}}\otimes\sigma_{p'_{2,j}})$  as

$$\left\langle \left\{ G_{i,j}(\sigma_{p'_{1,i}} \otimes \sigma_{p'_{1,j}}) \left( G_{i,j} \right)^{\dagger} \right\} \otimes \left\{ G_{i,j}(\sigma_{p'_{2,i}} \otimes \sigma_{p'_{2,j}}) \left( G_{i,j} \right)^{\dagger} \right\} \right\rangle_{G \in \Gamma}$$

$$= \sum_{p''} c_{p',p''} \left( \sigma_{p''_{1,i}} \otimes \sigma_{p''_{1,j}} \right) \otimes \left( \sigma_{p''_{2,i}} \otimes \sigma_{p''_{2,j}} \right). \tag{B.13}$$

Here  $G_{i,j} \in \Gamma = U(4)$  is a unitary gate and the Haar average over the gate set  $\Gamma$  has been taken. The coefficients  $c_{p',q'}$  are [34]

$$c_{p',p''} = \begin{cases} 0, & \left(p'_{1,i}, p'_{1,j}\right) \neq \left(p'_{2,i}, p'_{2,j}\right) \text{ or } \left(p''_{1,i}, p''_{1,j}\right) \neq \left(p''_{2,i}, p''_{2,j}\right), \\ 1, & \left(p'_{1,i}, p'_{1,j}\right) = \left(p'_{2,i}, p'_{2,j}\right) = \left(p''_{1,i}, p''_{1,j}\right) = \left(p''_{2,i}, p''_{2,j}\right) = (0,0) \\ \frac{1}{15}, & \left(p'_{1,i}, p'_{1,j}\right) = \left(p'_{2,i}, p'_{2,j}\right) \neq (0,0) \text{ and } \left(p''_{1,i}, p''_{1,j}\right) = \left(p''_{2,i}, p''_{2,j}\right) \neq (0,0). \end{cases}$$
(B.14)

In words, unequal strings are mapped to zero in one step. When strings are equal and II they remain unchanged. When strings are equal and not II they are uniformly sprayed among the 15 non II configurations. (When the gate set is a subset of U(4) the first, unequal case, is given by 1 − ∆ where ∆ is the gap of the gate set. These unequal configurations die away exponentially, like the k = 1 discussion in the text. The last coefficient is also modified with no qualitative effect.)

By using this expression, we can estimate the time dependence both for local and 2-local circuits. We first focus on the local circuit.

Note that we can write the sum in eq. [\(B.12\)](#page-50-0) as

$$\langle U_{1j}U_{j1}U_{1j}^*U_{j1}^*\rangle = 4^{-N} + 2^{-N} \sum_{p'\neq 0} \gamma_t(p',p') + 2^{-N} \sum_{p'_1\neq p'_2} \gamma_t(p'_1,p'_2).$$
 (B.15)

We can bound the second and third terms separately. For the U(4) gateset the third term in the sum is set to zero after a single step of the random circuit. One can check that the terms in the second term have the same sign, so we can rewrite the second term as

<span id="page-51-1"></span><span id="page-51-0"></span>
$$(*2) = 4^{-N} \sum_{p' \neq 0} 2^{N} \cdot |\gamma_t(p', p')|$$
(B.16)

where p 0 is a sum of all 2<sup>N</sup> − 1 Pauli strings of I's and Z's. For each fixed p 0 , we have a Markov chain that starts at position p 0 (note that 2<sup>N</sup> · |γ0(p 0 , p0 )| = 1)and converges to a uniform distribution on 4<sup>N</sup> − 1 strings.

<span id="page-51-2"></span>
$$\lim_{t \to \infty} 2^N \cdot |\gamma_t(p', p')| = \frac{1}{4^N - 1}$$
 (B.17)

This implies that limt→∞(∗2) ≈ 4 <sup>−</sup><sup>N</sup> · 2 <sup>−</sup><sup>N</sup> , which is exponentially suppressed relative to the Haar value (= 4−<sup>N</sup> ). So we need only focus on the early time decay of this quantity. In particular to establish closeness to Haar for fixed we do not need to consider the final decay governed by the gap of the full Markov chain.

Because of the large number of configurations, and in particular the 15 possible two Pauli strings not equal to II, the return probability in this chain is small and can be ignored (up to fractional errors of 1/15). So we can think of the configuration space as a large tree. The slowest decaying N qubit strings are those with lots of II substrings and hence very few Z's. We can ignore the rare cases where the Z's are adjacent. Then the overlap [\(B.16\)](#page-51-1) decays with a rate proportional to the number of Z's. The estimate becomes analogous to that presented in section [5.1.](#page-25-0)

As in section [5.1](#page-25-0) we will find a time of order log <sup>N</sup> for the sum of the slowest terms to be small enough to get close to the Haar value. At this time each of the slowest chains, those with a few Z's, have only spread to polynomially many other chains. So the gap of the full Markov process should not come into play.

The initial decay rate of the Pauli string with d Z's will be given by e <sup>−</sup>∆˜ <sup>d</sup> per step of the process where we write the probability for a two qubit string to remain in a given non II configuration as  $e^{-\tilde{\Delta}} = \frac{1}{15}$ . Therefore we have

$$(*2) = 4^{-N} \sum_{p' \neq 0} 2^{N} \cdot |\gamma_t(p', p')| \approx 4^{-N} \sum_{d=1}^{N} {N \choose d} e^{-\tilde{\Delta}dt}$$
 (B.18)

$$=4^{-N}\left((1+e^{-\tilde{\Delta}t})^{N}-1\right)$$
 (B.19)

Putting everything together and assuming N is sufficiently large, we conclude

$$\langle U_{1j}U_{j1}U_{1j}^*U_{j1}^*\rangle \approx 4^{-N}(1+Ne^{-\tilde{\Delta}t}) \qquad (j \neq 1).$$
 (B.20)

That is the strings with just one Z dominate this decay.

# Estimating $\left\langle U_{ii}U_{ii}U_{jj}^{*}U_{jj}^{*}\right angle$

We consider i=1, without loss of generality. Again in the 0,1-basis,  $|1\rangle$  is expressed by  $|0^N\rangle$ . In the same way as the discussion below (5.17), we take the state  $|j\rangle$  to be of the form  $|1^q0^{N-q}\rangle$ .

The argument is a simple generalization of the one for  $\langle U_{ii}U_{jj}^* \rangle$ , presented below (5.17). We start from the initial state on two copies of the system,

$$\hat{O}^{(2)}(0) = \left( \left| 0^N \right\rangle \left\langle 1^q 0^{N-q} \right| \right) \otimes \left( \left| 0^N \right\rangle \left\langle 1^q 0^{N-q} \right| \right). \tag{B.21}$$

The time evolution of  $\hat{\rho}$  is defined by

$$\hat{O}^{(2)}(t) = \left( U(t) \left| 0^N \right\rangle \left\langle 1^q 0^{N-q} \right| U^{\dagger}(t) \right) \otimes \left( U(t) \left| 0^N \right\rangle \left\langle 1^q 0^{N-q} \right| U^{\dagger}(t) \right). \tag{B.22}$$

Then, by construction,

$$U_{11}U_{jj}^*U_{11}U_{jj}^* = \text{Tr}\Big[\hat{O}^{(2)}(t)\hat{O}^{(2)\dagger}(0)\Big].$$
 (B.23)

We expand  $\hat{O}^{(2)}$  by Pauli strings as

$$\hat{O}^{(2)}(t) = 2^{-N} \sum_{p_1, p_2} \gamma_t(p_1, p_2) \sigma_{p_1} \otimes \sigma_{p_2}.$$
(B.24)

The coefficients at t = 0 are given by

$$\gamma_0(p_1', p_2') = 2^{-N} (+i)^{f(\vec{\alpha}(p_1')) + f(\vec{\alpha}(p_2'))},$$
(B.25)

when  $p'_1$  and  $p'_2$  are Pauli strings whose first q elements are X or Y and the remaining N-q elements are I or Z. For other  $p_1$  and  $p_2$ ,  $\gamma_0(p_1,p_2)=0$ . We can now write

<span id="page-52-0"></span>
$$U_{11}U_{jj}^*U_{11}U_{jj}^* = \sum_{p_1', p_2'} \gamma_t(p_1', p_2')\gamma_0^*(p_1', p_2')$$
(B.26)

We can split the sum into three parts

$$\langle U_{11}U_{jj}^*U_{11}U_{jj}^*\rangle = 4^{-N}\delta_{1j} + \sum_{p'\neq 0} \gamma_t(p', p')\gamma_0^*(p', p') + \sum_{p'_1\neq p'_2} \gamma_t(p'_1, p'_2)\gamma_0^*(p'_1, p'_2).$$
 (B.27)

The strings we have chosen have q X or Ys. We denote the number of Z's by d, where d can runs from zero to n-q. When  $j \neq 1$  or equivalently  $(q \geq 1)$  we note that for every string with an X at a given position there is an identical string with a Y at that position. Within the second term, contributions of string with X and Y in the same position will differ by a sign, due to the fact that there are two copies of the system and each one will contribute an i for each copy of Y. So the contributions of all such strings cancel exactly. This implies that when  $j \neq 1$  the second term in (B.27) will be set to zero in one step. Again for the U(4) gateset, the third term in the sum is also set to zero after a single step of the random circuit.

In the remaining case (j = 1), we will only have strings of I and Z and the evolution will be analogous to the previous section. In fact we not need to repeat this estimate because there is only one such term, i = j = 1, which makes an exponentially small contribution to the final value of u(2,t).

#### Summing up all the contributions

We can rewrite the sum as (recall  $L = 2^N$  and assume t > 1)

$$u(2,t) = \left\langle |\text{Tr}U^{2}(t)|^{2} \right\rangle = L \sum_{j=1}^{L} \left\langle U_{11}U_{11}U_{jj}^{*}U_{jj}^{*} \right\rangle + 2L \sum_{j=2}^{L} \left\langle U_{1j}U_{j1}U_{1j}^{*}U_{j1}^{*} \right\rangle.$$

$$\lesssim \frac{1}{L} \left( 1 + N \cdot e^{-\tilde{\Delta}t} \right) + 2 \left( 1 + N \cdot e^{-\tilde{\Delta}t} \right)$$

$$\simeq 2 + 2N \cdot e^{-\tilde{\Delta}t}. \tag{B.28}$$

The factor of 2 comes from the i, j symmetry in the sum. Note that this result becomes  $\epsilon$  close to the Haar value in times  $\frac{1}{\lambda} \log(N/\epsilon)$ .

For the 2-local system the above bound goes through without modification. There is one subtlety however. The results of Harrow-Low [34] show that full Markov chain convergence occurs in order  $\log N$  time, indicating that the entire Pauli string configuration space is covered in that time. So the tree approximation may no longer be valid. But by this time our quantity is exponentially close to the Haar value (B.17) and so there should be no increase in the estimate.

#### Alternate strategy

Another method for calculation the u(2,t) is to use the rewrite presented in equation (6.2),

$$u(2,t) = \left\langle \operatorname{Tr} U_t^2 \cdot \operatorname{Tr} (U_t^{\dagger})^2 \right\rangle = 1 + \sum_{j=2}^{L^2} \frac{1}{L} \left\langle \operatorname{Tr} \left( U_t^2 P_j (U_t^{\dagger})^2 P_j \right) \right\rangle$$
 (B.29)

where the sum is over all the non-identity Pauli strings. In the second term of the above formula, we translate the average over product of circuits to an average on two copies of the system followed by a swap operation  $\mathcal{F}$ .

$$\left\langle \operatorname{Tr}\left(U_t^2 P_j (U_t^{\dagger})^2 P_j\right) \right\rangle = \left\langle \operatorname{Tr}\left[\left((U_t P_j U_t^{\dagger}) \otimes (U_t^{\dagger} P_j U_t)\right) \mathcal{F}\right] \right\rangle$$
(B.30)

We have not analyzed this systematically, but observe that averaging can be done independently for each gate and so a new, perhaps more complicated, chain will result. The question then is to understand how coefficients of the Pauli strings decay in this chain for each application of a gate. If we ignore backtracking and approximate the chain evolution by a tree, as above, the question reduces to computing the decay rate for single gate from U(4).

The average of the U(4) gateset on a non-identity Pauli string AB (A, B = I, X, Y, Z) dotted with the same string can be computed and is,

$$\int_{\mathrm{U}(4)} dU \, \mathrm{Tr}\left(U^2 A B (U^{\dagger})^2 A B\right) = \frac{1}{15} \,. \tag{B.31}$$

This means that in the local and 2-local circuit the strings with d non-identity elements will decay like  $e^{-d\tilde{\Delta}t}$ , where again  $e^{-\tilde{\Delta}}=1/15$ . This indicates that the decay is dominated by the slowest decaying terms which have d=1. The total number of such terms is 3N, giving the log N result described above.

There is a small subtlety here. There are d=2 terms with the two Paulis adjacent to each other. These decay like a d=1 term. However there are only O(N) terms of this kind so this will just produce an addition to the numerical factor in front of N. This is a random circuit version of the factorization issue discussed in section 6. Therefore, the Haar time scaling,  $\sim \log \frac{N}{\epsilon}$ , is unaffected.

### <span id="page-54-0"></span>C Random band matrix

#### <span id="page-54-1"></span>C.1 Hamiltonian evolution

We consider an  $L \times L$  'band matrix' M with width w. Rather than introducing a sharp cutoff, we introduce a Gaussian suppression factor,

$$M_{ij} = M_{ij}^{(0)} \cdot \frac{e^{-\frac{(i-j)^2}{2w^2}}}{\sqrt{w}},$$
 (C.1)

where  $M_{ij}^{(0)}$  is the standard GUE ensemble.

By definition, banded random matrix becomes usual RMT at  $w \gtrsim L$ . It is not chaotic when the band is too narrow, namely at  $w \lesssim \sqrt{L}$ . In the following we consider large  $w \geq \sqrt{L}$ .

By using Y with appropriate value of  $\alpha$ , we can see the emergence of the ramp. As shown in figures 26 and 27, if we plot  $(w/L)^2|Y^2|$  against  $(w/L)^2 \cdot t$ , we can see a convergence to a smooth curve in a wide range, across the onset of the ramp. Therefore, regardless of the detail of the procedure to define  $t_{\text{ramp}}$ , it must scale as  $t_{\text{ramp}} \sim (L/w)^2$ . This scaling was derived in [25], where the authors showed that the energy scale at which sine kernel description of the two-point function breaks down is the same as the Thouless energy, which is the inverse of the time-scale for a local perturbation to diffuse through the entire system, namely  $t_{\text{diff}}$ . Note that the earlier growth of  $(w/L)^2|Y^2|$  is slower,  $\sim t^{1/2}$ . Our numerical plots are consistent with this scaling, as can be seen in figure 27.

It would be instructive to mention the difference between the banded matrices and local or k-local Hamiltonians. The band matrix mimics a Hamiltonian acting on L-state

#### <span id="page-56-0"></span>C.2 Brownian circuit

The Brownian circuit of random band matrices can describe diffusion, because the number of particles — which is one — is conserved. For this reason, as we will see, we can confirm  $t_{\text{Haar}} \sim (L/w)^2 \sim t_{\text{diff}}$ .

The numerical procedure is the same: we calculate  $U(t) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} e^{-iH_k dt}$ , where  $t = n \cdot dt$  and  $H_k$  are  $L \times L$  random band matrices with a width w. From this we calculate the  $\langle |TrU|^2 \rangle$ .

First let us see that the specific choice of dt is not important. When dt is sufficiently small, if we take  $tdt = n(dt)^2$  to be the horizontal axis, the dt-dependence is gone; see figure 28. This scaling can be understood if the time evolution is described as a random walk with step-size wdt. After n steps, the typical distance from the starting point should be  $\sqrt{n}wdt$ , if the random walk picture is true. Then for fixed L and w the dt-dependence should disappear when the horizontal axis is  $n(dt)^2$ . Below we fix dt to be 0.5.

In figure 29 we have plotted  $\langle |\text{Tr} U|^2 \rangle - 1$  against  $(t(w/L)^2)/(L^2-1)$  for fixed w=25 and various L, and for a fixed L/w. In both cases we can see an exponential decay. The exponent is a function of L/w, as we can see from the right panel of figure 29.

As we can see from figure 30, we can show the exponential decay at late time as well,  $\langle |\text{Tr} U|^2 \rangle - 1 \sim e^{-2t(w/L)^2}$ . Note that the exponent is different from the early time. If we define  $t_{\text{Haar}}$  as the time  $\langle |\text{Tr} U|^2 \rangle - 1$  reaches to a certain value, say 0.1, then this scaling leads to  $t_{\text{Haar}} \sim (L/w)^2$ .

It is possible to understand this scaling from the random walk picture. When the average distance from the starting point, which is  $\sqrt{t}w$ , is of order L, it is natural to expect that the transfer matrix U is almost a random unitary. Therefore  $\sqrt{t_{\text{Haar}}}w \sim L$ , or equivalently,  $t_{\text{Haar}} \sim (L/w)^2$ .
