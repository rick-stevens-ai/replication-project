\section{Paper}
E.\ Campbell, ``A random compiler for fast Hamiltonian simulation,''
\emph{Phys.\ Rev.\ Lett.}\ \textbf{123}, 070503 (2019). arXiv:1811.08017.

\section{Paper Summary}
qDRIFT is a stochastic alternative to Trotter--Suzuki product formulas for
simulating time evolution under $H = \sum_j h_j H_j$ with $h_j > 0$ and $H_j$
unitary (typically Pauli-like) terms. Instead of cycling deterministically
through all $L$ terms, qDRIFT samples each gate independently: term $j$ is
drawn with probability $p_j = h_j / \lambda$ where $\lambda = \sum_j h_j$, and
each sampled term is evolved for a fixed small angle $\tau = \lambda t / N$.

The headline result: the gate count $N$ to reach diamond-norm error
$\varepsilon$ scales as
\[
  N \;\approx\; \frac{2\,\lambda^{2}\,t^{2}}{\varepsilon},
\]
which is \emph{independent of $L$}. Deterministic first-order Trotter, by
contrast, pays an explicit dependence on $L$ (and on commutator structure).
The practical consequence is that qDRIFT is favourable for Hamiltonians with
many small terms (large $L$, modest $\lambda$), e.g.\ quantum chemistry
Hamiltonians.

\section{Methods and Substitutions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{System:} $n = 4$ qubits (dim 16), exact dense simulation.
        Random Hamiltonian $H = \sum_j h_j P_j$ with $P_j$ random Pauli
        strings, coefficients rescaled so that $\lambda = \sum_j h_j = 4.0$
        is held \emph{fixed} while $L \in \{8, 24, 60\}$ varies. This
        isolates the $L$-dependence cleanly: as $L$ grows, the individual
        $h_j$ shrink but $\lambda$ is preserved.
  \item \textbf{Ground truth:} exact $U = \exp(-iHt)$, $t = 0.5$, via
        \texttt{scipy.linalg.expm}.
  \item \textbf{Trotter baseline:} deterministic first-order product formula
        with $r$ repetitions (gate count $= L \cdot r$). Higher-order
        Suzuki formulas (Trotter-2, Trotter-4) were \emph{not} implemented ---
        a genuine gap noted in the critique.
  \item \textbf{qDRIFT:} $N$ independent samples, term $j$ drawn
        $\propto h_j$, each evolved for angle $\tau = \lambda t / N$. Channel
        error estimated by averaging output-state trace-norm deviation over
        600 samples $\times$ 4 random input states.
  \item \textbf{Error metric:} $\tfrac{1}{2} \| \rho_{\mathrm{out}}
        - U \rho U^{\dagger} \|_{1}$ averaged over input states (a
        diamond-norm proxy). qDRIFT's analytic bound is diamond norm; our
        proxy sitting \emph{below} the bound is consistent.
  \item Seed \texttt{20260626} for reproducibility. Artifacts:
        \texttt{replicate.py}, \texttt{results.json}, \texttt{results.csv},
        \texttt{error\_vs\_gates.png}, \texttt{run.log}.
\end{itemize}

Error $\times N$ is roughly constant (i.e.\ $1/N$ scaling) and the measured

\subsection{Trotter comparison}
Deterministic first-order Trotter, smallest error per $L$:
$L = 8 \to 8.1\!\times\!10^{-3}$ at $N = 256$;
$L = 24 \to 2.8\!\times\!10^{-3}$ at $N = 768$;
$L = 60 \to 1.2\!\times\!10^{-3}$ at $N = 1920$.
Trotter's gate count to reach a target precision grows with $L$ (gates
$= L \cdot r$), whereas qDRIFT reaches comparable precision at an $N$ set by
$\lambda^{2} t^{2} / \varepsilon$ independent of $L$ --- reproducing the
qualitative advantage for many-term Hamiltonians.

\section{Critique (Honest)}

\textbf{What was genuinely and independently exercised.} The two headline
quantitative claims of the paper --- $1/N$ error scaling and $L$-independence
at fixed $N$, both bounded by $2 \lambda^{2} t^{2} / N$ --- were reimplemented
clean-room from \texttt{numpy}/\texttt{scipy}, without consulting or executing
any code from the paper's authors. Every tested quantitative prediction held
within Monte Carlo noise, and the measured error sits strictly below the
analytic bound at every gate count.

\textbf{Where the replication is thin.} Several honest gaps must be flagged:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Trotter baseline is minimal.} We compared against
        \emph{first-order} Trotter only. Campbell's paper (and follow-ups)
        show the more competitive comparison is against Trotter-2 or
        Trotter-4 (Suzuki fractals), which scale better in $t$ and can beat
        qDRIFT in regimes with structured, low-commutator Hamiltonians. Our
        ``qDRIFT beats Trotter'' claim is therefore only reproduced against
        the weakest baseline; the paper's more nuanced regime comparison
        against Trotter-2/4 was not exercised.
  \item \emph{Small qubit count.} $n = 4$ (dim 16) is set by exact-simulation
        cost. The $L$-independence and $1/N$ scaling are algorithmic
        properties, so we expect them to persist, but we have not exercised
        the regime the paper actually cares about (tens to hundreds of
        qubits, thousands of Pauli terms in molecular electronic-structure
        Hamiltonians).
  \item \emph{Chemistry resource estimates unreproduced.} The paper's
        headline application is chemistry Hamiltonians (e.g.\ H$_{2}$O,
        FeMoco-style estimates). We did not reproduce any of those specific
        gate-count estimates because (a) the concrete Pauli coefficient sets
        are described but not deposited as machine-readable data, and
        (b) even given those, the exact simulation of the resulting
        many-qubit dynamics is infeasible classically. This is the largest
        substantive gap.
  \item \emph{Diamond-norm proxy, not diamond norm.} We compute an
        average-case trace-norm-on-Haar-random-input error and compare it to
        the paper's diamond-norm (worst-case) bound. The fact that our
        proxy is bounded above by the diamond-norm bound is consistent, but
        it is not a check on the tightness of the bound; a genuine
        diamond-norm computation on the qDRIFT channel would require SDP
        machinery we did not deploy.
  \item \emph{No noise-robustness / open-system extension.} We simulated
        ideal unitary sampling. Whether qDRIFT's $L$-independence advantage
        survives realistic 2-qubit gate noise (depolarizing, coherent
        overrotation, cross-talk) is out of scope and non-trivial.
  \item \emph{Random Pauli Hamiltonians only.} Our Hamiltonians are random
        Pauli strings with rescaled random weights. Structured Hamiltonians
        (lattice models, molecular integrals) may have very different
        commutator structure and hence different Trotter-vs-qDRIFT
        crossovers.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Bottom line.} The paper's core algorithmic claims are quantitatively
reproduced end-to-end. The chemistry resource estimates and the
regime-dependent comparison against high-order Trotter are \emph{not}
reproduced. Given qDRIFT is a pure algorithm paper, and every numerically
testable core claim matched tightly, this reaches the REPLICATED tier ---
but coverage is limited by classical-simulation cost, not by any observed
disagreement.
