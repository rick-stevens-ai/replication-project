\begin{abstract}
This is a backfill report for the LUCID replication of Cordoni (2023), \emph{Entropy} 25:1322, DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.3390/e25091322}{10.3390/e25091322}.
The paper derives a van~Kampen system-size expansion of the Generalized Stochastic Microdosimetric Model (GSM\textsuperscript{2}) and proves that the resulting lethal-lesion distribution is strictly \emph{sub-Poissonian} (Fano factor $<1$).  All 13 testable claims across three figures were reproduced by our Gillespie SSA + linear-noise (LNA) implementation at the paper's single parameter set ($x_0=100$, $y_0=0$, $r=4$, $a=0.1$, $\tilde b_K = 0.01$).  Headline number: Fano$(Y)\approx 0.685\pm 0.005$ from both SSA and LNA.  Verdict: \textbf{REPLICATED}.  This report preserves the earlier findings and adds an honest critique section that flags a real, unavoidable limitation: because the paper reports \emph{no raw numerics} and only one parameter set, the agreement is between our own two implementations built from the paper's equations; we cannot cross-validate against author code or an author-supplied dataset, and the sub-Poissonian regime tested here corresponds to a \emph{low}-clustering parameter set that does not stress-test the theory in the high-LET/high-clustering regime where the underlying Poisson-in-DSBs picture is known to break down.
\end{abstract}

\section{Paper summary}

Cordoni (2023) starts from the microdosimetric master equation (MME) for the GSM\textsuperscript{2} of Cordoni, Missiaggia et al.\ (\emph{Phys.\ Rev.\ E} 103:012412, 2021).  A cell nucleus carries two populations,
\begin{itemize}
  \item $X$ = number of sub-lethal lesions,
  \item $Y$ = number of lethal lesions,
\end{itemize}
evolving under three elementary reactions:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
$X \to \varnothing$ & rate $r$ & (sub-lethal repair) \\
$X \to Y$ & rate $a$ & (direct conversion) \\
$2X \to Y$ & rate $\tilde b_K \equiv b/K$ & (pairwise clustering).
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The MME is non-linear (in $X$) because of the clustering term, and no closed form for $p(t,y,x)$ is known.

The paper performs a \emph{van~Kampen system-size expansion} under the ansatz
\[
X(t) = K\bar x(t) + \sqrt K\,\xi(t), \qquad Y(t) = K\bar y(t) + \sqrt K\,v(t),
\]
and identifies two layers in the large-$K$ limit:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Order $\sqrt K$ (Eq.~11): the deterministic MKM equations,
  \[
  \dot{\bar x} = -(a+r)\bar x - 2b\bar x^2, \qquad \dot{\bar y} = a\bar x + b\bar x^2.
  \]
  \item Order $1$ (Eq.~14): a linear 2-D Fokker--Planck equation for $(\xi,v)$; the solution is a bivariate Gaussian whose covariance matrix $(c_{\xi\xi}, c_{\xi v}, c_{vv})$ obeys the ODE system in Eq.~16.
\end{enumerate}
From the integral identity Eq.~18,
\[
c_{vv}(t) = \bar y(t) - \delta(t), \qquad \delta(t) = -\int_0^t 2(2b\bar x(s) + a)\, c_{\xi v}(s)\, ds \ge 0,
\]
because $c_{\xi v}\le 0$ (a rise in $Y$ can only come from a drop in $X$).  Therefore $\mathrm{Var}(Y)<\mathbb E[Y]$: the lethal-lesion distribution is strictly \emph{sub-Poissonian}.  This is the paper's headline analytical result.

The paper also gives (a) a non-truncated FPE representation (Remark~4, Eq.~23) and (b) an equivalent time-dependent Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) representation (Remark~3, Eq.~22).  Section~5 shows numerically that the linear-noise approximation tracks the full SSA except very close to the absorbing boundary $\bar x \to 0$.

\section{Claims tested}

The paper contains three kinds of claims:
\begin{description}
  \item[A --- Structural (analytic).] The van~Kampen expansion of the MME reproduces the MKM ODEs at order $\sqrt K$ and yields a linear 2-D FPE with Gaussian solutions at order $1$.
  \item[B --- Qualitative long-time behavior.] For the chosen parameters (Sec.~4): $\bar x(t)\to 0$; $\bar y(t)\to \bar y_\infty>0$; $c_{\xi\xi}(t)\to 0$; $c_{vv}(t)\to c_{vv,\infty}>0$; $c_{\xi v}(t)\le 0$ for all $t>0$ and $\to 0$ as $t\to\infty$; and $c_{vv}(t)<\bar y(t)$ (sub-Poissonian).
  \item[C --- Numerical (Figs.\ 1--3).] The LNA Gaussian PDF approximates the SSA histograms of $X$ and $Y$ at $t\in\{0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ a.u.; OU sample paths straddle the deterministic mean similarly to SSA paths.
\end{description}

\section{Method (our implementation)}

Full source: \verb|code/gsm2_model.py| and \verb|code/run_replication.py|.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gillespie SSA.} Direct-method Gillespie for the CTMC with propensities $r X$, $a X$, $\tilde b_K X(X-1)$.  20\,000 paths, integrated over $t\in[0,1.5]$ a.u.\ on 301 evenly spaced time points.  Seed: \verb|np.random.default_rng(20260529)|.
  \item \textbf{Macroscopic ODE (Eq.~11).}  \verb|scipy.integrate.solve_ivp| with LSODA, \verb|rtol=1e-10|.
  \item \textbf{Moment ODE (Eq.~16).}  Integrated jointly with the mean field so time-dependent drift coefficients are exact.
  \item \textbf{Linear-noise OU paths (Eq.~22).}  Euler--Maruyama on a 3010-point sub-grid ($dt \approx 5\times 10^{-4}$).  Diffusion matrix $D_{xx}=(a+r)\bar x+4b\bar x^2$, $D_{vv}=a\bar x+b\bar x^2$, $D_{xv}=-(2b\bar x^2+a\bar x)$; lower Cholesky factor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Critique --- what our replication does not, and cannot, prove}
\label{sec:critique}

This is deliberately \emph{not} a whitewash.  The paper is analytically strong and internally consistent, and our reproduction of its qualitative and numerical claims is clean.  But there are real limitations that a naive reader of the ``REPLICATED'' verdict should be aware of.

\paragraph{(C1) No author numerics; the agreement is between our own two implementations.}
The paper publishes \emph{no} raw numerical output --- no CSVs, no digitized figure data, no reference solver.  Both our SSA and our LNA are derived from the same set of equations (Eq.~11, 16, 22) in the same paper.  We are cross-checking two implementations of the same theory against each other, not against an independent oracle.  A shared derivation error (e.g.\ a sign in $D_{xv}$) would go undetected by this internal cross-check.  \emph{Partial mitigation:} the SSA is a direct implementation of the CTMC (no expansion, no truncation) while the LNA is the leading-order asymptotic of that same CTMC, so a systematic error in the expansion \emph{would} produce mean/variance disagreement --- and we see none.  But this is weaker than a comparison to author code.

\paragraph{(C2) The single parameter set is deep in the well-behaved regime.}
The paper reports Sec.~4 numerics only for $(x_0, r, a, \tilde b_K) = (100, 4, 0.1, 0.01)$.  In this regime the clustering rate $\tilde b_K \bar x^2$ is small compared to the linear terms, sub-lethal lesions dominate the dynamics, and the LNA is in its comfort zone.  The paper's own warning (its Fig.~1 top-right at $t=0.9$ where $\bar x\approx 1.7$) is that the LNA breaks down as $\bar x\to 0$, i.e.\ when the discrete Markov chain has $\mathcal O(1)$ molecules and the Gaussian ansatz fails.  Our replication reproduces this failure mode exactly, but neither the paper nor this replication probes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{high clustering} (large $\tilde b_K$ or small $r$): the regime where non-linearity dominates and higher-order van~Kampen corrections may be non-negligible;
  \item \textbf{small $x_0$} (initial lesion count $\mathcal O(1)$): the discrete/quantized regime where the Gaussian ansatz for fluctuations is not even asymptotically valid;
  \item \textbf{dose sweeps}: no $D$-dependence is exposed because the paper treats $x_0$ as a given initial condition rather than as a mapping from physical dose.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(C3) The Poisson-in-DSBs picture is not tested at high LET.}
The task brief flags this explicitly, and we agree it is the single most important open question.  The paper's headline is about deviation from Poisson at the level of \emph{lethal lesions} $Y$, not at the level of primary DSBs.  The primary DSB count in real radiobiology is empirically \emph{not} Poisson at high LET, where DSBs are spatially clustered on scales below the nuclear volume and the induction distribution is measurably over-dispersed (negative binomial or worse; see e.g.\ Ballarini \& Ottolenghi 2005, Friedland et al.\ 2017, MC track-structure simulations with PARTRAC/Geant4-DNA).  Cordoni's model \emph{assumes} the input $X_0$ is deterministic (or at least that its distribution is not the object of study) and derives sub-Poissonian output for $Y$ from the clustering-plus-repair dynamics alone.  This is a valid and interesting result --- but the reader should not conclude that it validates or invalidates the Poisson assumption for DSB induction at high LET.  It sits one layer downstream.  A concrete test would be: initialize $X_0\sim\mathrm{NegBin}(\mu, \phi)$ with $\phi$ chosen to match a high-LET track-structure prediction (e.g.\ $\bar\phi \approx 2$--$5$ for iron ions), rerun the SSA, and see whether the sub-Poissonian $Y$ result survives or is swamped by over-dispersion of the input.  We did not do this; the paper does not do this.

\paragraph{(C4) The identifiability of $(r,a,b)$ from data is untouched.}
The paper picks $(r,a,\tilde b_K)=(4,0.1,0.01)$ ``for illustration.''  These three rate constants are not independently measurable from any published cell-survival or foci-count dataset that we know of.  Without an identifiability analysis (Fisher information, profile likelihood, or at minimum a sensitivity table), the paper's numerical results are illustrative rather than predictive.  This is not a criticism of the derivation --- it is a criticism of treating the model as ready for radiobiological application.

\paragraph{(C5) Repair-kinetics variance and cell-to-cell heterogeneity are absent.}
The three rate constants $(r,a,b)$ are assumed constant in time and identical across cells.  Real repair kinetics are (i) two-phase (fast NHEJ + slow HR/backup), (ii) cell-cycle-phase dependent, and (iii) heterogeneous across a population.  The MME framework can in principle absorb time-dependent rates, but the paper does not do so.  Neither did we.  Whether the sub-Poissonian conclusion survives such extensions is genuinely unknown from this work.

\paragraph{(C6) No comparison to real foci-count data.}
$\gamma$-H2AX or 53BP1 foci counts are routinely reported as histograms per cell in the radiobiology literature (Rothkamm \& L\"obrich 2003; Costes et al.\ 2010; Barnard et al.\ 2013).  Some of those datasets \emph{are} publicly available.  A confirmatory test --- ``does the Fano$<1$ prediction survive against a real dataset at matched dose?'' --- is entirely outside the paper's scope and outside our replication.  The paper's contribution is theoretical; treating our clean 13/13 verification as evidence that the model matches biology would be a category error.

\paragraph{(C7) Numerical caveats.}
$20\,000$ SSA paths is generous for mean/variance of a bounded output but marginal for tail probabilities.  A serious test of the Gaussian tails --- e.g.\ estimating $P(Y\ge \bar y + 3\sqrt{c_{vv}})$ against the LNA prediction --- would need $10^5$--$10^6$ paths and was not performed.  The Fig.~1 histograms are consistent with Gaussianity by eye but we have not run a formal test (Anderson--Darling on the residuals against $\mathcal N(\bar y, c_{vv})$; would be $\mathcal O(1)$~hour of additional work).

\medskip
None of these limitations undermine the paper's mathematical result.  They do bound the strength of the ``REPLICATED'' verdict: what we have reproduced is the internal mathematical consistency of the paper at its single reported parameter set, not its predictive validity as a radiobiological model.
