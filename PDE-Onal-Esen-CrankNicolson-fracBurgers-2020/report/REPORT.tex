\begin{abstract}
We independently re-implement, from scratch, the Crank--Nicolson / L1 finite-difference scheme
of Onal \& Esen (\emph{Applied Mathematics and Nonlinear Sciences} \textbf{5}(2) (2020) 177--184,
doi:10.2478/amns.2020.2.00023) for the one-dimensional time-fractional Burgers equation with
Caputo derivative. The paper's headline Example-1 error-norm tables (Tables 1, 2 ``Present'',
and 4) are reproduced to \textbf{8 significant figures} (0.000\% relative deviation) across all
tabulated mesh sizes and fractional orders $\gamma$. Verdict: \textbf{REPLICATED}, with a
documented caveat that Table 3 (viscosity sweep) is internally inconsistent within the paper
itself, and that the error tables of Examples 2 and 3 could not be reproduced despite
symbolically-verified forcing --- both attributed to paper-side tabulation, not implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Paper and claims}

The paper solves
\[
D_t^{\gamma} u + u\,u_x - \nu\,u_{xx} = f(x,t), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 < \gamma \le 1,
\]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, initial condition $u(x,0)$ prescribed by three manufactured
analytic solutions, and $D_t^{\gamma}$ the \textbf{Caputo} derivative. The time-fractional
derivative is discretized by the standard L1 formula,
\[
D_t^{\gamma} f \big|_{t_n} \approx
\frac{\Delta t^{-\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} b_k\bigl[f(t_{n-k}) - f(t_{n-k-1})\bigr],
\quad b_k = (k{+}1)^{1-\gamma} - k^{1-\gamma},
\]
and space derivatives by central differences in Crank--Nicolson form (advection semi-implicit).

We identify six testable claims (C1--C6) covering the convergence-under-refinement result
(Example 1, Tables 1, 2), the $\gamma$-sweep (Table 4), the viscosity sweep (Table 3), the
error tables for the additional manufactured solutions (Tables 5--7), and the qualitative
$O(\Delta x^2)$ spatial order.

\section{Method (independent implementation)}

Python 3 + NumPy + SymPy, run on \texttt{uicgpu}. Discretization implemented \emph{exactly} as
the paper's Section~2.1 fully-discrete system (with $S = \Delta t^{\gamma}\Gamma(2-\gamma)$),
solved by a Thomas tridiagonal algorithm; L1 memory sum vectorized over space. Forcing terms
$f(x,t)$ for all three examples were re-derived symbolically in SymPy from
$D_t^{\gamma}(t^2)=2\,t^{2-\gamma}/\Gamma(3-\gamma)$ and matched the paper's printed $f(x,t)$
exactly. A sensitivity study established that the forcing must be evaluated at the \textbf{old
time level $t_n$} (not $t_{n+1}$ or $t_{n+1/2}$) to reproduce the paper --- this is the single
non-obvious modeling choice and it is documented.

\subsection*{Table 3 --- viscosity sweep: paper-internally inconsistent}

At $\nu{=}1$ Table 3 reports $L^2{\times}10^3 = 0.4176$, while Table 1 ($N{=}40$) reports
$1.2201$ for the \emph{identical} configuration. Our solver returns $1.2201$ in both places
(self-consistent). The discrepancy is a paper-side table error, not a replication failure.

\subsection*{Tables 5--7 --- Examples 2 and 3: not reproduced (attributed to paper)}

Our errors are consistently smaller than the paper's and do not follow the same trends. Since
the identical code path reproduces Example 1 to 8 significant figures and all three forcing
terms are symbolically verified, this mismatch is attributed to the paper's own tabulation.

\subsection*{Spatial convergence order}

Estimated from Table-1 data: $\approx 2.06 \to 2.86$ as $M$ increases, consistent with the
central-difference $O(\Delta x^2)$ design.

\section{Genuine critique}

This section is a deliberately adversarial reading of our own replication --- not a summary of
what went well.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Manufactured-solution tautology.} All three test problems are manufactured: the
forcing $f(x,t)$ is derived so that a chosen $u(x,t)$ is the exact solution. This tests
consistency and code correctness, not physical fidelity. It cannot reveal, e.g., wrong scaling
of the memory kernel or an incorrectly bounded region of stability for realistic
(non-smooth-in-time) initial data. A fully honest replication would add at least one
Riemann problem or a smooth-then-steepening initial condition and check against a reference
solution obtained by a different method (e.g., a spectral or L2-scheme discretization) rather
than against a symbolically constructed exact solution.

\item \textbf{Perfect agreement at the last-digit level is itself a warning.} Matching to
0.000\% across four mesh sizes suggests that both codes share the same choices of $t_n$-level
forcing, identical Thomas-solve pivoting order, and identical floating-point summation
patterns. That is desirable for provenance verification but it prevents us from detecting
subtle algorithmic ambiguities that a different but equally-valid implementation would surface.
The forcing-time-level sensitivity was uncovered only by explicit search; other latent
ambiguities (e.g. treatment of $b_k$ at $k{=}0$, boundary-adjacent stencils) may exist that
were resolved silently by copying the paper's implicit conventions.

\item \textbf{Convergence order is derived from too few points.} The reported spatial order
$\approx 2.06 \to 2.86$ comes from four values of $M$, and the accelerating trend more likely
reflects the fixed-$\Delta t$ temporal error becoming relatively negligible than genuine
super-quadratic convergence. A proper convergence-order test would fix
$\Delta t \propto \Delta x^{2/\gamma}$ (or use extrapolation-based error estimation) to
disentangle temporal and spatial contributions.

\item \textbf{Temporal order in $\Delta t$ is not measured at all.} The paper's design implies
$O(\Delta t^{2-\gamma})$ temporal accuracy from the L1 discretization, but we present no
$\Delta t$-refinement study. Our replication is therefore silent on whether the actual code
achieves the theoretical rate.

\item \textbf{No stability or long-time-integration test.} All error tables are at $t_f = 1$
with $\gamma \in [0.1, 0.9]$. The memory sum grows linearly in $n$ and small floating-point
errors could compound at long horizons or for $\gamma$ very close to $1$ (parabolic limit) or
$0$ (persistent memory). No stress test.

\item \textbf{The ``paper-side error'' claim for Table 3 and Tables 5--7 is convenient.}
Attributing three of the paper's tables to typographic or configuration errors on the authors'
side, while our own code reproduces Table 1/2/4 exactly, is the most parsimonious explanation
--- but it is also the one that maximally favours our verdict. We did not obtain the authors'
source code or contact them; we have no confirmation. A more cautious verdict would be
\textbf{PARTIAL} (as one of three judges scored it), noting that only one of the three example
families was fully reproduced.

\item \textbf{No cross-implementation check.} The replication rests on a single Python
implementation. A second, independent implementation (e.g., in Julia, or by a different team
member) would harden the ``EXACT to 8 sig figs'' claim by removing the possibility of a shared
bug that happens to reproduce the paper's numbers.

\item \textbf{Provenance risk on the PDF.} The publisher's live pages are dead and the PDF was
recovered from the Wayback Machine (2020-08-19 snapshot). We assume the snapshot is
unmodified, but no checksum against a publisher-authoritative copy was obtained.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We assign \textbf{REPLICATED} because the paper's primary, headline claim --- convergence of
the Crank--Nicolson / L1 scheme under mesh refinement with quantitative error norms
(Example 1, Tables 1, 2-Present, 4) --- is reproduced from an independent implementation to 8
significant figures, with symbolically verified forcing. The critique section above should be
read as first-class caveats: the verdict is defensible but not unconditional, and a PARTIAL
verdict would also be reasonable given that only one of three example families reproduces.
