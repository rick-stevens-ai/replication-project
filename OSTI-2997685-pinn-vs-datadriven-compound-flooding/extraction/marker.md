# A Comparative Study of Physics-Informed and Data-Driven Neural Networks for Compound Flood Simulation at River-Ocean Interfaces: A Case Study of Hurricane Irene

Dongyu Feng<sup>1</sup>, Zeli Tan<sup>1</sup>, Zihan Lin<sup>2</sup>, Donghui Xu<sup>1</sup>, Cheng-Wei Yu<sup>3</sup>, and QiZhi He<sup>2</sup>

**Abstract** Simulating compound flooding (CF) at the river-ocean interface within large-scale Earth System Models (ESMs) presents significant challenges due to complex interactions between river discharge, storm surge, and tides. This study assesses the comparative advantages of physics-informed and data-driven machine learning (ML) approaches for enhancing local ESM performance. We systematically compare data-driven neural network models (i.e., CNNs, U-Net, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit), and physics-informed neural network (PINN) models, including vanilla PINN and a finite-difference-based PINN (FD-PINN). Specifically, FD-PINN is introduced to enhance computational efficiency, accelerating vanilla PINNs by ~6.5 times while improving accuracy. To enhance data-driven model training, a new data-generation approach is developed to sample historical fluvial and coastal flood events, which ensures a robust data set for extreme event prediction. The models are evaluated using a realistic one-dimensional river domain extracted from an ESM's river mesh and the Hurricane Irene event as an independent test case. Results show that FD-PINN achieves accurate predictions with significantly reduced computational costs relative to vanilla PINNs. Among data-driven models, the best overall performance is achieved by a CNN-LSTM hybrid, which balances accuracy and efficiency. While a fully connected CNN (CNN-FC) provides the best accuracy, it incurs high computational cost. Architectures lacking strong temporal modeling tend to underperform on unseen events. These findings highlight the importance of sequence-aware designs for robust generalization. This study reveals the trade-offs between physics-informed and data-driven models and proposes an adaptive hybrid framework for integrating ML into ESMs to enhance local flood simulations.

Plain Language Summary Compound flooding often happens in coastal regions when heavy rain, tides, and storm surges occur together. Predicting these complex events is very difficult, especially for large climate models that simulate the Earth's systems. In this study, we explore how machine learning (ML) can help improve flood simulations in these critical coastal areas. We tested two different types of ML approaches: (a) physics-informed models, which follow the rules of physics, and (b) data-driven models, which learn from past flood data. We introduced a new version of a physics-informed model, called FD-PINN, and a new way to create better training data for data-driven models. Using a real-world river section and the case of Hurricane Irene, we found that the improved physics-informed model can simulate compound flooding accurately while using much less computing time. Among data-driven models, a hybrid design combining two types of ML (CNN and LSTM) worked best at balancing accuracy and efficiency. Our study shows that combining physics and data-driven methods can offer powerful new tools to improve flood simulations in climate models, helping communities better prepare for future extreme weather.

#### 1. Introduction

Compound flooding (CF) arises from nonlinear interactions of multiple flood drivers of extreme river discharge, storm surge, and tide, particularly at river-ocean interfaces (Feng, Tan, Xu, & Leung, 2023; Santiago-Collazo et al., 2019; Wahl et al., 2015; Zscheischler et al., 2020). These interactions amplify flood risks in low-lying coastal regions (Bevacqua et al., 2019), where coastal backwater effects from tide and storm surge can impede river discharge or even reverse flow direction (Feng, Tan et al., 2022; Olbert et al., 2023). CF is projected to intensify under climate change (Sarhadi et al., 2024), with recent studies documenting increased frequency and intensity of such events globally (Eilander et al., 2020). Earth System Models (ESMs) are comprehensive tools designed to simulate the interconnected processes of the Earth's atmosphere, land, rivers, and oceans (Golaz et al., [2019,](#page-23-3) [2022\)](#page-23-4) (Figure [1a](#page-1-0)). While ESMs have advanced in coupling riverine and coastal processes (Feng et al., [2024;](#page-23-5) Xu et al., [2022,](#page-26-3) [2024](#page-26-4)), they face critical challenges in accurately representing CF dynamics at the river‐ocean interface (Ward et al., [2020\)](#page-26-5). In particular, the current ESM framework employs one‐dimensional (1D) large‐scale river models and two‐dimensional (2D) or three‐ dimensional (3D) ocean models (Figure [1b](#page-1-0)), each optimized for their respective domains. Specifically, the 1D large‐scale river models, which are computationally efficient and suitable for channelized flow where pressure gradients dominate, employ reduced physics governing equations, such as kinematic wave or diffusion wave approximations of full dynamic Saint Venant equations (SVE) (Feng, Tan et al., [2022](#page-23-1); Ikeuchi et al., [2017](#page-24-0)). These simplifications cannot accurately represent inertial momentum in low‐lying regions (Hodges, [2013\)](#page-24-1). Additionally, despite variable‐resolution capabilities, these models cannot achieve the spatial resolution required to resolve fine‐scale processes within river channels due to inherent large‐scale modeling assumptions (Li et al., [2013](#page-24-2)). Conversely, ocean models, though capable of resolving detailed coastal dynamics with high‐ resolution meshes in estuaries, face their own challenges. Extending the computational domain of an ocean model upstream into river networks substantially increases computational demands through additional grid cells and smaller time steps (Lilly et al., [2023](#page-24-3)). Moreover, applying 2D/3D models to predominantly 1D channelized flow represents an inefficient use of computational resources (Hoch et al., [2017](#page-23-6)), and may introduce unnecessary complexity in regions where flow is primarily governed by along‐channel momentum.

Over the past decade, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising solution for addressing the computational and physical modeling challenges in flood prediction (Kratzert et al., [2018;](#page-24-4) Nearing et al., [2024;](#page-25-3) Tran et al., [2023\)](#page-25-4). In the context of ESMs, ML provides a pathway to incorporate subgrid‐scale processes into large‐ scale simulations, enabling efficient representations of flood dynamics across multiple spatial and temporalscales (Reichstein et al., [2019;](#page-25-5) Willard et al., [2022\)](#page-26-6). ML models can serve as computationally efficient alternatives that capture fine‐scale flood dynamics while maintaining numerical stability and physical consistency (Sit et al., [2020](#page-25-6)). By integrating into existing ESM frameworks (Camps‐Valls et al., [2021](#page-22-1)), ML models can serve as flexible intermediate‐scale models that improve the representation of river‐ocean coupling, which is critical for simulating extreme events.

ML applications have demonstrated success across various flooding scenarios, including river flooding (Jiang et al., [2022](#page-24-5); Nevo et al., [2022](#page-25-7)), coastal flooding (Dey et al., [2024](#page-23-7); Hasan et al., [2023\)](#page-23-8), urban flooding (Hou

Broadly, ML approaches can be categorized into two paradigms: data‐ driven models that infer system behavior purely from observational or simulation data and physics‐informed models that embed physical laws within learning algorithms. Each approach faces distinct trade‐offs in terms of accuracy, generalizability, and computational demands.

Data‐driven approaches have shown remarkable success in capturing complex spatial and temporal patterns in flood dynamics (Sit et al., [2020\)](#page-25-6). They offer substantial improvements in predictive accuracy while reducing computational costs compared with traditional numerical methods. The evolution of data‐driven models has progressed from simple statistical methods to sophisticated deep learning architectures, with each advancement offering new capabilities for handling the complexities of flood dynamics. Traditional ML methods, including Linear Regression (Tsakiri et al., [2018](#page-26-7)), Radial Basis Function (Panigrahi et al., [2019](#page-25-10)), Support Vector Regression (Panahi et al., [2021\)](#page-25-11), Decision Trees (Pham et al., [2021](#page-25-12)), and Random Forests (Chen et al., [2020\)](#page-22-2), have established foundational frameworks for flood prediction. Recent advances in computing power and algorithm design have enabled more sophisticated approaches, such as Gaussian Process Regression (Betancourt et al., [2020\)](#page-22-3) and gradient boosting methods such as XGBoost (Lee et al., [2024](#page-24-9)), which offer improved predictive capabilities while maintaining interpretability. Moreover, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have proven particularly effective in spatial feature extraction and flood extent mapping (Wang et al., [2020\)](#page-26-8) while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) excel at capturing temporal dependencies in flow dynamics (Graves & Graves, [2012](#page-23-10); Nearing et al., [2024](#page-25-3); Xiang et al., [2020\)](#page-26-9). These architectures have been successfully applied to various flood‐ related tasks, including water level or discharge predictions (Pan et al., [2020;](#page-25-13) Solanki et al., [2025\)](#page-25-14), flood forecasting (Le et al., [2019](#page-24-10)), 1D/2D flow simulations(Crompton et al., [2019;](#page-23-11) Maxwell et al., [2021;](#page-25-15) Pang, [2024;](#page-25-16) Zhang et al., [2024\)](#page-26-10), and flood inundation modeling (Kabir et al., [2020\)](#page-24-11). Recent studies have demonstrated the capability of data‐driven models to simulate compound flooding in river systems by incorporating both upstream discharge and downstream water level data (Moradian et al., [2024](#page-25-17)). Data‐driven models rely solely on historical data for training, potentially capturing complex phenomena that may be difficult to describe through physical equations alone (Yin et al., [2021](#page-26-11)). However, these approaches face challenges in extrapolating beyond their training data (Frame et al., [2022](#page-23-12)), particularly for extreme events such as peak streamflow conditions (Kim et al., [2021](#page-24-12)). This limitation necessitates careful consideration of training data selection and generation strategies (Fraehr et al., [2025\)](#page-23-13).

Physics‐Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) represent a novel method in physics‐informed ML, combining the universal approximation capabilities of neural networks with physical constraints derived from governing equations (Raissi et al., [2019\)](#page-25-18). In the context of flood modeling, PINNs offer a unique advantage by enforcing conservation laws and physical boundary and initial conditions directly within the neural network architecture, ensuring conservation of mass and momentum (Karniadakis et al., [2021\)](#page-24-13). Several variants of PINNs have emerged to address specific computational challenges: numerical method based PINNs leverage structured grid discretization to improve gradient computation accuracy and training stability (Jiang et al., [2023](#page-24-14); Lim et al., [2022](#page-24-15); Xiang et al., [2022](#page-26-12)). Self‐adaptive PINNs incorporating attention mechanisms have also been developed to better handle multi‐scale physics and complex boundary conditions (Xiang et al., [2021\)](#page-26-13). Recent applications of PINNs in water modeling have demonstrated promising results across various scenarios. These include groundwater flow (Cuomo et al., [2023\)](#page-23-14) and surface water dynamics using 1D kinematic wave equations (Niu et al., [2023\)](#page-25-19), diffusive wave equations (Hou et al., [2024\)](#page-24-16), dynamic wave equations (Dazzi, [2024;](#page-23-15) Feng, Tan, & He, [2023;](#page-23-16) Luo et al., [2024\)](#page-24-17), and 2D shallow water equations (Qi et al., [2024](#page-25-20); Tian et al., [2025](#page-25-21); Zhou et al., [2025\)](#page-26-14). The ability of PINNs to handle complex boundary conditions and maintain physical consistency makes them particularly suitable for modeling CF at river‐ocean interfaces. The key advantage of PINNs lies in their ability to incorporate physical knowledge directly into the learning process, potentially reducing the required training data while ensuring physically consistent solutions (Cai et al., [2021](#page-22-4)). In addition to learning the underlying hydrodynamics, PINN has the unique capability of assimilating observational data (He et al., [2020](#page-23-17); He & Tartakovsky, [2021\)](#page-23-18). However, challenges remain in terms of training stability, computational efficiency, and scalability to complex geometries (Cuomo et al., [2022\)](#page-23-19).

Despite their different approaches, both PINNs and data‐driven models offer complementary strengths for flood modeling. PINNs provide physically consistent solutions and require less training data but may be computationally intensive during training (Karniadakis et al., [2021](#page-24-13)). Data‐driven models offer faster training and inference times and can capture complex patterns from data but require extensive training data sets (Willard et al., [2022\)](#page-26-6).

They lack the ability to intrinsically incorporate physical laws, which may lead to non‐physical predictions under extrapolative conditions (Brunton & Kutz, [2022\)](#page-22-5). A comparison of these approaches in the context of CF modeling remains largely unexplored, particularly regarding their integration potential with ESMs (Jafarzadegan et al., [2023\)](#page-24-18).

This study presents a systematic comparison of physics‐informed and data‐driven neural networks for CF modeling, with a particular focus on hurricane‐induced CF during Hurricane Irene (2011). We use a small river reach (Lower Delaware River) extracted from a global ESM framework as a testbed to assess model performance and computational efficiency. To improve the efficiency of PINN, we devised an innovative architecture that uses the finite difference scheme to replace the computational expensive *automatic differentiation* (AD) in vanilla PINNs. We also developed a new training data generation approach that effectively captures extreme fluvial and coastal floods. This approach ensures that the data set is well‐suited for training data‐driven models. By evaluating different ML architectures, including vanilla PINN, finite‐difference‐based (FD) PINN, and various data‐ driven models, we aim to identify optimal approaches for simulating CF at the river‐ocean interface of ESMs.

# **2. Methods**

This study evaluates the performance of physics‐informed and data‐driven machine learning models to enhance the representation of hydrodynamic processes in the 1D channel at the interface between river and ocean components in ESMs (Figure [1a\)](#page-1-0). This 1D channel is extracted from the coarser‐resolution river model mesh used in ESMs and refined to a higher resolution suitable for machine learning applications. At this interface, the river and ocean models provide the upstream and downstream boundary conditions, respectively (Figure [2a](#page-4-0)). For this refined 1D domain, we implement two distinct ML modeling strategies:

- 1. Physics‐Informed Neural Network (PINN): The PINN‐based models are set up to take boundary condition (BC) information from ESMs, along with available observational or synthetic data, to provide a high‐ resolution hydrodynamic solution.
- 2. Data‐Driven Neural Networks: data‐driven models require a high‐resolution numerical model to generate training data. A hydrodynamic model is set up to simulate historical flood events, producing a data set of spatiotemporal water depth variations under different boundary conditions. This data set is then used to train various data‐driven models (CNN, U‐Net, Long Short‐Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)), which learn statistical relationships between input boundary conditions and flood dynamics.

By testing these models, we aim to assess their advantages and limitations in resolving the compounding hydrodynamic processes. The objective is to determine the most effective approach for integrating ML into ESMs to improve CF simulations at finer scales.

#### **2.1. Physics‐Informed Neural Networks (PINN)**

Here, we first introduce the vanilla PINN model (hereafter, standard PINN) (Feng, Tan, & He, [2023](#page-23-16); Raissi et al., [2019](#page-25-18)) to solve the one‐dimensional (1D) Saint‐Venant equations (SVE), which describe unsteady, gradually varied open‐channel flow. PINN is a mesh‐free deep learning framework that embeds the SVE as soft constraints in the loss function, ensuring that the learned solutions satisfy physical laws. Unlike purely data‐driven models, PINNs do not require pre‐generated training data sets from numerical simulations. We apply several enhancements, including adaptive weighting (Wang et al., [2021](#page-26-15)), Fourier feature embedding (Wong et al., [2022\)](#page-26-16), and *L*<sup>2</sup> regularization (Wang et al., [2023\)](#page-26-17), to improve the accuracy and stability of the PINN framework (Feng, Tan, & He, [2023](#page-23-16)).

PINN approximates the solution to these equations using a deep neural network (DNN). The input layer consists of spatial (*x*) and temporal (*t*) coordinates while the output layer predicts the unknown state variables *h*(*x*,*t*, *θ*) and *u*(*x*,*t*, *θ*), where *θ* is the vector of weights and biases. The governing equations are enforced as soft constraints using AD (Abadi et al., [2016](#page-22-6)), ensuring that the learned solution satisfies the underlying physics. The neural network architecture for PINN consists of a fully connected feed‐forward DNN with *L* hidden layers, each containing *NL* neurons.

In this study, the designed PINN network uses the tanh activation function, which has been found to improve gradient propagation in physics‐based learning problems. The network parameters (weights and biases) are optimized using the Adam optimizer with an exponentially decaying learning rate. The total loss function in our PINN framework is formulated as

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}(\theta) + \sum_{j} w_{j} \mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta) + L_{\text{reg}}, \tag{4}$$

<span id="page-5-0"></span>where LPDE denotes the loss term of the governing PDE's residuals, L*<sup>j</sup>* represent loss contributions from boundary conditions and various data sources, including upstream and downstream boundaries (i.e., at *x*<sup>0</sup> and *xn*), remote sensing snapshots at the revisit time of satellites (i.e., *t*<sup>0</sup> and *tm*), and in situ time‐series data at the available gauges (Figure [2b](#page-4-0)). Lreg denotes the regularization term. Both gauged time series measurements and spatial snapshots from remote sensing can be incorporated into the PINN training via these additional loss terms. While PINNs can, in principle, be trained using only initial and boundary conditions, the high‐resolution initial conditions are not available in the targeted domain of ESMs. Thus, the remote sensing data are used to constrain the spatial evolution of the solution.

The weights *wj* are used to balance the contributions of each corresponding L*j*. A critical challenge in training PINNs is to balance different loss terms as the scales of residuals from PDEs, boundary conditions, and data constraints can vary significantly. Fixed weighting schemes often lead to imbalanced issues, where one term dominates the loss function, causing poor convergence. To address this, we implement an adaptive weighting strategy based on the gradient balancing method (Wang et al., [2021](#page-26-15)). The weighting coefficients *wj* are updated dynamically at each training step to balance the gradients of different loss terms. The value of *wj* at the *k*‐th step of gradient descent during the training is provided as

$$w_j = (1 - \alpha)w_j + \alpha \frac{\max_{\theta} |\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{PDE}(\theta_k)|}{|\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_j(\theta_k)|},$$

where the overbar denotes the mean with respective to *θ*. The hyperparameter *α* is 0.9 following (Jin et al., [2021\)](#page-24-19). This ensures that all components of the loss function contribute meaningfully to the optimization process, preventing any single term from dominating the training. The last term in Equation [4](#page-5-0) (Lreg) is the *L*<sup>2</sup> regularization term (Girosi et al., [1995\)](#page-23-20):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \lambda \sum_{j} w_{j}^{2},\tag{6}$$

where *λ* is a regularization coefficient. This term is used to penalize large weights in a neural network and prevent discontinuity or overfitting (Wang et al., [2023\)](#page-26-17).

Instead of directly using *x* and *t*, we map them into a Fourier‐transformed feature space

#### 2.2. Finite-Difference-Based (FD) PINN

Despite their theoretical advantages, standard PINNs often suffer from a lack of training efficiency due to the intensive use of AD for calculating derivatives. This computational bottleneck limits their scalability, particularly in high-resolution simulations of hydrodynamics. To address these limitations, we introduce the Finite-difference-based PINN (FD-PINN) following Lim et al. (2022) and Jiang et al. (2023), which replaces AD with finite-difference numerical approximations of spatial and temporal derivatives (Figure 2b). Here, we use a second-order central difference method

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \frac{f(x, t + \Delta t) - f(x, t - \Delta t)}{2\Delta t},\tag{8}$$

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{f(x + \Delta x, t) - f(x - \Delta x, t)}{2\Delta x},\tag{9}$$

where  $\Delta x$  and  $\Delta t$  are the step sizes and f denotes the flow variables u and h. Strict stability conditions are not required in FD-PINN as in traditional numerical solvers, since the governing equations in PINNs are applied as soft constraints. Stability-related issues can still arise. For example, the use of unnormalized flow variables with normalized space and time inputs can lead to unrealistically large PDE residuals and divergence during initial iterations.

Unlike standard PINNs that rely on AD and thus require traversing the full computational graph to compute PDE residuals, FD-PINN evaluates these residuals using explicit finite-difference formulas. This approach reduces memory usage and can accelerate training by avoiding the overhead of repeated AD operations, particularly in high-resolution settings. FD-PINN also enhances numerical stability by mitigating errors associated with vanishing or exploding gradients, particularly in high-gradient regions. However, both standard PINNs and FD-PINNs still rely on backpropagation for optimizing network parameters. Although the FD approach introduces mesh discretization, in contrast to the continuous nature of AD-based differentiation, it retains the core structure and training logics and parameters as standard PINNs. While finite-difference approaches have been previously introduced into PINN frameworks in other disciplines (e.g., for problems in electromagnetics or heat transport) (Jiang et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2022), this study presents the first application of FD-PINN to the SVE for hydrodynamic modeling. By combining physics-informed learning with improved computational efficiency, FD-PINN provides a practical and scalable solution for river-coastal applications and integration into ESMs.

#### <span id="page-6-0"></span>2.3. Data-Driven Neural Networks

This study develops and evaluates a suite of data-driven neural networks for CF dynamics in the 1D river-ocean domain, including convolutional models, a hybrid CNN-LSTM model, a U-Net encoder-decoder architecture, and recurrent models (LSTM and GRU). Unlike PINNs, these models do not incorporate governing equations explicitly but instead rely on learning statistical relationships between boundary forcing and flood response from simulated hydrodynamic data.

The neural networks are trained to map input boundary conditions to output water depth distributions along the 1D domain. The selection of the model architectures is based on their effectiveness in capturing different hydrodynamic features relevant to CF modeling. CNN1D (referred as CNN throughout this study), a one-dimensional

convolutional neural network, captures spatial correlations in flood wave propagation efficiently (Atashi et al., [2023](#page-22-7)). We evaluated three CNN‐variants: CNN‐FC, a fully connected CNN with a large dense output layer; CNN‐Conv, a simplified fully convolutional model using 1D convolutions and a linear projection layer; and CNN‐LSTM, a hybrid architecture combining convolutional feature extraction with temporal sequence modeling using LSTM layers. These variants represent different design trade‐offs between complexity, efficiency, and temporal expressiveness. U‐Net, a fully convolutional network with an encoder‐decoder architecture and skip connections, is well‐suited for capturing multi‐scale flood dynamics (J. Li et al., [2024\)](#page-24-20). LSTM and GRU, both RNN architectures, are selected due to their wide applications in streamflow predictions, where they effectively capture temporal dependencies in time series data (Le et al., [2021](#page-24-21); Nevo et al., [2022\)](#page-25-7). LSTM is designed to handle sequential dependencies and is effective in modeling long‐term flood memory, while GRU, a variant of LSTM with fewer parameters, provides faster training while maintaining comparable predictive capability but may lose some expressiveness in capturing highly nonlinear flood dynamics. Each model is trained using a supervised learning approach, with the loss function minimizing the difference between predicted and simulated water depth. The trained models are evaluated based on prediction accuracy, computational efficiency, and generalization performance on an unseen extreme event.

#### **2.3.1. Data Set Generation**

To ensure a robust and representative training data set, we develop a structured data processing and sampling approach that enhances the model's ability to generalize to extreme flood events (Figure [2c](#page-4-0)).

A key limitation of training ML models on the full streamflow and water level time series is the imbalance between frequent low‐to‐moderate floods and rare extreme floods, which can lead to biased predictions and poor generalization to extreme events (Kim et al., [2021\)](#page-24-12). To address this, we developed a new method for training data generation (Figure [2c](#page-4-0)). This method generates synthetic training data using high‐resolution hydrodynamic simulations, in which long‐term observational records are used to inform the statistical characteristics of boundary forcing.

We first analyze historical flood records of river discharge at the upstream boundary and ocean water level at the downstream boundary to identify realistic and representative flood scenarios (Feng, Tan et al., [2022](#page-23-1)). For the selection of fluvial flood events from the gauged discharge time series, peak values are first identified using the peaks‐over‐threshold (POT) method (Lang et al., [1999](#page-24-22)). The threshold is determined through an automated selection process, ensuring that only independent flood peaks are retained (Zhang et al., [2021](#page-26-18)). To further ensure independence, a declustering technique is applied to eliminate closely spaced peaks. The selection of the storm surge event is performed by (a) extracting the storm surge component from the hourly total water level data and (b) using a peak detection algorithm to filter the extreme events (Feng, Tan et al., [2022](#page-23-1)). This method identifies periods where peak river discharge and extreme coastal storm surges occur within a given time window. These events are then ranked based on intensity of peak discharge and storm surge level, and a return period analysis is performed to ensure the inclusion of only significant flood scenarios. To focus on extreme conditions, we select events with a return period exceeding 5 years. This ensures that the training data set is not biased toward more frequent moderate floods.

Rather than using these historical events directly, we generate synthetic boundary conditions through Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) (Iman, [2008\)](#page-24-23). Each sampled event represents a distinct realization of riverine and coastal forcing. The training data set thus captures a broad spectrum of possible CF conditions. We also introduce timing shifts to create additional variability while preserving physically realistic magnitudes. The synthetic combinations are then used as boundary forcing inputsto a high‐resolution hydrodynamic model, which simulates water depth evolution over the event duration. A withheld extreme event is excluded from the training data set to assess model generalization on an unseen event during testing. This approach can produce a physically consistent, diverse, and statistically grounded data set for training data‐driven models.

#### **3.2. Numerical Model Setup and Data Collection**

In the extracted 1D river‐ocean domain, a high‐resolution hydrodynamic model was configured using the TELEMAC‐MASCARET modeling system (Feng et al., [2025](#page-23-21); Hervouet, [2000](#page-23-22)) to provide both training and reference data for the ML models. Although the global E3SM also encompasses this region (Feng et al., [2024](#page-23-5)), its 10‐day Hurricane Irene simulation cannot sample the full spectrum of CF scenarios needed for robust model training. TELEMAC is selected for its ability to solve the full dynamic SVE and its efficiency to run on high‐ performance computing (HPC) systems. The latter is important for generating the large ensemble of simulations needed to train and evaluate data‐driven models. The model was configured with a spatial resolution of 200 m in both the along‐channel and cross‐channel directions, where the river topology data are from Feng et al. [\(2024](#page-23-5)) (Figure [3;](#page-8-0) Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1). Although the original setup was two‐ dimensional (2D), careful evaluation confirmed that variations in the cross‐channel direction were negligible, which justifies the reduction to a 1D framework while preserving the essential flood dynamics.

To perform TELEMAC simulations over various historical events and generate the ensemble training data sets, the upstream and downstream boundary conditions were obtained from the closest NOAA and USGS gauge stations, respectively (Figures [3;](#page-8-0) Figures [4a](#page-9-0) and [4b](#page-9-0)). The model was run for 10 days, and the output was archived at 1‐hr intervals. The simulation was first spun up for 1 month, after which a restart file was saved. This restart condition was then used to run an ensemble of event‐scale simulations, generating a diverse and physically consistent training data set for the data‐driven models.

#### <span id="page-9-1"></span>**3.3. Machine Learning Model Setup**

In the PINN and FD‐PINN training, the synthetic remote sensing data at the initial and last time steps, assuming a 10‐day revisiting time, and synthetic in situ times‐series observations over the simulation period are from the TELEMAC simulation. The in situ observation sites are imposed at three evenly distributed locations along the channel (Figure [2a](#page-4-0)). While the in situ measurements are generally considered highly accurate (Turnipseed & Sauer, [2010\)](#page-26-19), remote sensing data, even when refined to resolutions on the order of 10 m, such as SWOT data (Domeneghetti et al., [2018](#page-23-23); Getirana et al., [2024](#page-23-24)), may introduce greater uncertainties. To evaluate the robustness of the PINN framework to observational noise, we imposed noise levels of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% on the synthetic remote sensing data. For both PINN and FD‐PINN, the collocation points where the PDE residuals are evaluated were uniformly distributed with a spatial resolution of 400 m and a temporal resolution of 1 hr, which ensures sufficient resolution for model accuracy while avoiding excessive computational overhead during training. The architecture of PINN and FD‐PINN consists of a fully connected feed‐forward DNN with five hidden layers, each containing 64 neurons. We employed the widely used initialization scheme of Xavier scheme (Glorot & Bengio, [2010](#page-23-25)). This configuration follows the design used in Cases 5 and 6 of Feng, Tan, and He [\(2023](#page-23-16)), which were trained with a comparable amount of data. The models are trained for 80,000 iterations,

For the data-driven models, the training data set was constructed following the methodology outlined in Section 2.3. Fluvial flooding and storm surge events were first identified from the 30-year time series of available data, considering that the NOAA gauge was established in 1989 (Figures 5a and 5c). To define the downstream boundary conditions, the measured total water level was decomposed into its tidal and storm surge components using harmonic tidal analysis with eight tidal constituents (M<sub>2</sub>, S<sub>2</sub>, N<sub>2</sub>, K<sub>2</sub>, K<sub>1</sub>, O<sub>1</sub>, P<sub>1</sub>, Q<sub>1</sub>, and M<sub>4</sub>). The storm surge component was then subjected to event-based sampling, and the selected events were superimposed onto the tidal signal to reconstruct realistic boundary conditions.

The selected flooding events were ranked based on their return periods, and only those exceeding 5 years were included in the training data set (Figures 5b and 5d). Hurricane Irene, which has a return period of approximately 10 years in terms of both discharge and storm surge, was intentionally excluded from the training data set to serve as an unseen test event. For model evaluation, the data-driven models were assessed on the full test data set as well as two subsets: one comprising events with a return period below 10 years (representing less extreme floods) and another with return periods exceeding 10 years (representing more extreme events). This enables a comprehensive assessment of model performance across different flood magnitudes.

For each selected event, the peak timing was shifted by 1–5 days within the 10-day period, generating a total of 88 fluvial flood samples (Figure S3a in Supporting Information S1), 96 storm surge (Figure S3b in Supporting Information S1), and downstream water level samples (Figure S3c in Supporting Information S1). The 10-day duration was chosen to be consistent with the Hurricane Irene simulation used in the PINN experiments and in Feng et al. (2024). This duration reflects the typical timescale of CF events in the Lower Delaware River. In such events, storm surge often determines the duration as it typically occurs over just a few days. We found the 10-day

window to be sufficient for capturing the interaction between fluvial and coastal forcings. Using the LHS-sampled boundary conditions, a total of 800 ensemble simulations were conducted (Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1). To assess the impact of training data set size on model performance, the data-driven models were trained using subsets ranging from 100 to 800 simulations. The data set was then partitioned into 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing.

In this study, we applied a two-step logarithmic transformation and Min-Max scaling approach to preprocess the input and output features for the data-driven models. Given the wide range of values and potential skewness in the data set, particularly between discharge and water level, natural logarithm transformation with a shift was first applied to the boundary conditions and target variables

$$\xi' = \log(1+\xi),\tag{11}$$

where  $\xi$  represents discharge and water depth and  $\xi'$  denotes the transformed data. This transformation helps to reduce the impact of extreme values and stabilize the variance of the data. Following the logarithmic transformation, we applied Min-Max scaling to normalize the transformed variables into the range (0.01, 1):

$$\xi_{\text{scaled}} = \frac{\xi' - \xi'_{\text{min}}}{\xi'_{\text{max}} - \xi'_{\text{min}}} \times (1 - 0.01) + 0.01, \tag{12}$$

where  $\xi_{\text{scaled}}$  is the scaled data and  $\xi'_{\text{max}}$  and  $\xi'_{\text{min}}$  represents the maximum and minimum values in  $\xi'$ , respectively. This scaling ensures that the data remain within a controlled range while avoiding zero values, which can be problematic for NN activations. Separate Min-Max Scalers were fitted for the two input features and the output feature to ensure that each feature was normalized independently to preserve the original distribution characteristics, particularly effective for deep learning applications in hydrodynamic modeling.

The architectures of the data-driven models, including the number of hidden layers, neurons per layer, and inputoutput shapes, are provided in Figure S5 of Supporting Information S1. All models are trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the objective function. Model configurations were optimized through trial and error based on the size and characteristics of the available training data set. To support generalization to unseen extreme events, we emphasized maintaining architectural simplicity across all models, especially for the more complex model U-Net that is more prone to performance degradation in such cases. Finally, in addition to the  $L_2$  norm, we evaluated the performance of all ML models using MSE, Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and coefficient of determination ( $R^2$ ). For efficiency comparison, all models are trained using Tensorflow on an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6148 CPU @ 2.4 GHz.

#### 4.1. Standard and FD PINNs

The predicted water depth from both standard PINN and FD-PINN is evaluated against the TELEMAC reference solution for Hurricane Irene (Figure 6). Both models show relatively low error, with  $L_2$  norms of 0.037 and 0.035, respectively, both values being lower than those reported in Cases 5 and 6 of Feng, Tan, and He (2023) (Figure 7). However, both models struggle to fully capture the precipitation-induced high discharge from the upstream boundary in the sloped region (x = 65,000–70,000 m). A larger bias is also observed in the transition zone (x = 55,000–60,000 m), where nonlinear tide-surge-discharge interactions occur. This discrepancy is expected, as handling discontinuities remains a well-known challenge for PINNs, particularly in scenarios involving shock waves with strong nonlinear dynamics (Liu et al., 2024).

# **5. Discussions**

## **5.1. Intercomparison Between Physics‐Informed and Data‐Driven Neural Networks**

The comparison of PINN, FD‐PINN, and data‐driven ML models highlights differences in efficiency, interpretability, and computational requirements, offering distinct advantages depending on application needs (Table [1](#page-17-0)). PINNs leverage governing equations as soft constraints in the loss function, allowing solutions without requiring pre‐generated training data sets from numerical simulations of a high‐fidelity model. When remote sensing or observational data are available, PINNs can be trained solely on these data, making them particularly valuable where a high‐resolution model has not been set up. However, in the absence of satellite observations, high‐resolution numerical outputs may be used as synthetic observations, but these are not an inherent requirement of the framework. While this approach is feasible, it reduces the practical advantage of the PINN framework by introducing dependency on physical models, which can limit its independence and scalability across diverse regions.

PINNs are computationally expensive, with training times on the order of O(10 hr) in this case, limiting their scalability. FD‐PINNs improve upon standard PINNs by incorporating finite‐difference discretization, significantly enhancing training efficiency while maintaining physics constraints. With training times reduced to O (1 hr), FD‐PINNs overcome one of the main challenges of standard PINNs while retaining interpretability. However, they require a structured numerical mesh, which adds complexity to the setup. Data‐driven ML models offer the fastest inference time, requiring only O(1 min) to O (1 hr) for training and very low (O(1 min)) post‐ training computational costs compared to ∼10 min per TELEMAC run, making them ideal as lightweight surrogates for rapid scenario exploration. While PINNs and FD‐PINNs typically use multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), data‐driven models vary in architecture, depending on the task and data structure. Also, data‐driven models such as CNN, LSTM, and GRU have demonstrated improved accuracy over PINNs in our study, showing their suitability for predicting flood dynamics.

Another key distinction between these approaches lies in model deployment efficiency. Both PINNs and FD‐ PINNs follow an online training paradigm, meaning that once trained, they can generate solutions instantly without requiring additional simulations. In contrast, data‐driven models depend on extensive pretraining with labeled data, which is typically generated from numerical simulations. Setting up these simulations requires model configuration, mesh generation, BC processing, and input data preprocessing, along with prior expertise in numerical modeling. Additionally, running numerical simulations can be highly time‐consuming. For instance, generating training data for this study required 800 TELEMAC simulations, each taking 10 min, totaling 133 hr. Training times for data‐driven models also vary significantly: LSTM and GRU complete training in just a few minutes while CNN‐FC requires over an hour when trained on 800 samples (Figure S13 in Supporting Information S1). These computational demands highlight the upfront efforts required to train purely data‐driven models, which are highly case‐specific and depend on factors such as region, spatial scale, model setup complexity, and the user's background and domain knowledge. However, once trained, data‐driven models can deliver near‐instantaneous predictions, making them particularly effective for repeated runs under perturbed or uncertain inputs.

#### **5.2. Implication for ESM Implementation**

#### <span id="page-18-0"></span>**5.2.1. Benchmarking Against an ESM Proxy**

To provide a baseline for evaluating the performance of ML models against traditional physics‐based approaches, we set up two cases using HEC‐RAS (Brunner et al., [2015](#page-22-10)) within the same domain extracted from our global E3SM simulation (Figure [3\)](#page-8-0). HEC‐RAS was selected as a proxy for the river component of an ESM because it offers a diffusion wave method, consistent with those used in large‐scale ESM river models, for example, E3SM (Feng, Tan et al., [2022](#page-23-1)). HEC‐RAS was configured using the same input parameters as the TELEMAC and ML model setups, including channel slope, geometry, roughness, and boundary conditions. No additional calibration of the model was performed as it only serves as a benchmark representative of ESMs rather than training data. We employed two spatial resolutions (Δ*x* = 2,000 m and 5,000 m), which are among the highest resolutions available in ESMs. Comparison of ESM predictions with the TELEMAC reference (Figure S14 in Supporting Information S1) indicates that coarser resolutions introduce significant errors in simulating CF dynamics in this region. While the ESM model can capture the propagation of tides and storm surges, it struggles to accurately simulate areas dominated by upstream discharge and the transition zone where tide‐surge‐discharge interactions occur. This is due to unresolved flow dynamics, particularly in regions where bathymetric changes cause rapid shifts in flow regimes. Such ESM performance is expected to degrade further in regions with more complex hydrodynamic conditions.

At three locations representing different hydrodynamic regimes—tidal‐dominated (*x* = 10,000 m), transition zone (*x* = 60,000 m), and discharge‐dominated (*x* = 65,000 m)—we evaluated the ESMs (Figures [12a–12c\)](#page-19-0), PINNs (Figures [12d–12f](#page-19-0)), and data‐driven models trained on 100 and 800 samples (Figures [12g–12l](#page-19-0)) against the TELEMAC reference solution. The assessment was based on bias (i.e., MAE) and the ability to capture temporal variation (i.e., *R*<sup>2</sup> ) (Figure [13\)](#page-19-1).

The comparisons highlight a significant improvement of ML‐based models over ESMs. In the tidal zone, all three approaches effectively capture the time series variations driven by tide and storm surge (Figures [12a–12d,](#page-19-0) 12g and [12j\)](#page-19-0). However, PINNs, especially FD‐PINN, show substantial improvements over ESMs in the transition and discharge‐dominated regions, due to their combined use of physics‐informed constraints and assimilated observational data. Notably, the largest bias during peak discharge events in the discharge‐dominated region is reduced by 0.5 and 1.5 m, compared with ESMs at 2,000 m and 5,000 m resolutions, respectively (Figures [12c](#page-19-0) and [12f](#page-19-0)). Among data‐driven models, those trained on 800 samples (except CNN‐Conv and U‐Net) achieve higher accuracy in these regions (Figures [12h,](#page-19-0) 12i, 12k and [12l](#page-19-0)). However, as these models rely entirely on training data without physics constraints and data assimilation, their predictions do not correlate as well as PINNs. This is further reflected in MAE and *R*<sup>2</sup> values at *x* = 60,000 and 65,000 m (Figure [13](#page-19-1)), where PINNs show larger MAEs than data‐driven models but achieve higher *R*<sup>2</sup> values, indicating better alignment with observed temporal variations.

## **5.2.2. Toward Integration Into Earth System Models**

The FD‐PINN algorithm and the data generation schemes for data‐driven models are highly adaptable and can be applied to any domains affected by compound riverine and coastal flood drivers. These ML models can be

seamlessly integrated into an ESM framework through model couplers (Larson et al., [2005\)](#page-24-26), albeit in slightly different ways, providing a more flexible and adaptive tool.

FD‐PINN functions as a PDE solver with online training, making it well‐suited for direct integration as a new ESM component. It receives inputs from the river and ocean components of the ESM, solves the governing equations at the given coupling interval, and returns the solution to other components through the coupler. The operational scale of FD‐PINN aligns with that of a global ocean component, enabling smooth interaction within the ESM framework. In contrast, data‐driven models rely on pretraining with historical data and perform rapid inference when provided with new inputs. This distinction highlights the advantage of using ML as a lightweight and scalable alternative for embedding fine‐scale dynamics within ESMs, particularly in scenarios where deploying high‐resolution hydrodynamic solvers across multiple local regions is computationally impractical and requires extensive effort for model setup and modeling expertise.

Since ML models predict much faster than physics‐based ESM components, they can be coupled by assigning a shorter coupling interval. ML models can be incorporated into ESMs through a Fortran‐Python interface (Zhang et al., [2024](#page-26-10)) or online data exchange APIs. Integrating these ML models into ESMs not only provides more resolved and accurate CF dynamics in localized regions but also improves boundary conditions for its upstream and downstream components. This integration effectively overcomes current ESM limitations in predicting extreme events, providing a more precise representation of flood interactions across scales.

#### **5.3. Limitations and Future Work**

#### **5.3.1. Current Limitations and Challenges**

While the developed FD‐PINN has demonstrated significant efficiency over the standard PINN, this study solely focuses on the efficiency improvement brought by the algorithm itself to enable a fair comparison with the data‐ driven models trained on the same CPU. The training efficiency can be further improved without much burden by leveraging GPU acceleration (Sharma & Shankar, [2022\)](#page-25-23), which can be up to two times faster than CPU‐based training (Lu et al., [2024\)](#page-24-27). Further enhancements, such as adaptive training strategies or hybrid PINN architectures that incorporate domain decomposition (Jagtap & Karniadakis, [2020](#page-24-28)), could improve the scalability. Implementing transfer learning techniques, where parameters (such as weights and biases) trained on one CF event are used as initial parameters for another event, could also reduce training time (Daramola et al., [2024](#page-23-27); Lippert et al., [2024;](#page-24-29) Prantikos et al., [2023\)](#page-25-24).

Thisstudy utilizes a realistic river domain extracted from E3SM'sriver mesh and applies a real hurricane scenario. The current SVE formulation assumes a rectangular channel geometry as this choice is consistent with the configuration of the river component of E3SM (Feng, Tan et al., [2022;](#page-23-1) Li et al., [2013\)](#page-24-2). Given the minimal width variation in our study domain, this simplification is reasonable. However, we acknowledge that in cases with significant cross‐sectional variability or widespread flooding (Fuller et al., [2013\)](#page-23-28), a variable channel width or cross‐sectional area or even 2D SWE approximation may be needed to better capture the flow dynamics and improve generalizability. Moreover, this study is limited to Hurricane Irene trained with synthetic TELEMAC simulations as the comparative framework is intended as a foundational step toward broader applications. We note, however, that applying models trained on one numerical model (e.g., TELEMAC) to another would introduce inter‐model discrepancies that can complicate interpretation of ML performance. For this reason, we retain TELEMAC as a consistent baseline in the present study. Future work should extend the comparative framework to other compound flood events and evaluate model performance across independent high‐fidelity models and real observational data sets. Such studies will be essential for testing robustness and operational readiness.

This study relies on the synthetic data generated from the TELEMAC simulations. This approach was necessary because, although the PINN framework (Figure [2a\)](#page-4-0) is robust, high‐resolution remote sensing data, for example, from SWOT, are not yet available. As a result, the experimental design was structured around simulated training data. While this allows for controlled benchmarking, it does not fully capture the complexity and uncertainty of real‐world observations. Our noise sensitivity analysis indicates that PINNs begin to degrade significantly when noise exceeds 5%. This provides a practical benchmark for assessing real‐world data. Data sets with noise lower than this threshold may be suitable for integration, whereas higher‐noise observations may reduce framework effectiveness. Moving forward, future research should focus on incorporating real satellite observations, such as

FENG ET AL. 21 of 27

those from SWOT, Landsat, and Sentinel, which will require preprocessing to generate high‐quality satellite data products (Rostami et al., [2024](#page-25-25); Schumann et al., [2023\)](#page-25-26). Such integration is essential to validate the models' generalizability and operational readiness.

The data‐driven models in this study are trained solely on synthetic data sets generated from hydrodynamic simulations without assimilating observational data, which is different from the training of PINNs. While this approach ensures full control over training data quality and consistency, it does not take advantage of real‐world satellite observations that could be used to constrain or enhance predictions. Incorporating such observational inputs would likely require alternative model designs (e.g., hybrid data–model fusion, sequence‐based assimilation) (Nearing et al., [2021;](#page-25-27) Penny et al., [2022](#page-25-28)), which remain a promising direction for future research. Integrating observational constraintsinto data‐driven models could also enable fairer comparisons with PINNs, which explicitly assimilate satellite snapshots in our framework.

This study builds upon previous successes and incorporates multiple strategies to achieve satisfactory performance. However, PINN is an actively evolving framework, with ongoing developments introducing new methods that could further improve its effectiveness, such as Neural‐Integrated Meshfree (NIM) method (Du & He, [2024\)](#page-23-29) that combines differentiable programming with meshfree discretization methods, adaptive sampling strategies to refine collocation point selection (Zhao et al., [2024](#page-26-21)), physics‐informed attention mechanisms to enhance feature extraction (Jiang et al., [2025\)](#page-24-30), and Multi‐Scale Deep Neural Networks (MscaleDNNs) that addresses multi‐scale frequency features (Huang et al., [2025\)](#page-24-31). Additionally, developing hybrid models that combine PINNs with data‐ driven approaches could provide a balanced trade‐off between computational efficiency and physical interpretability in CF modeling (Zhang et al., [2023](#page-26-22)).

While this study focuses on model accuracy and efficiency, interpretability, and uncertainty quantification are equally important for real‐world applications. Future work could incorporate explainable AI techniques, such as SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, [2017](#page-24-32)) or Layer‐wise Relevance Propagation (Bach et al., [2015](#page-22-11)), to improve transparency in data‐driven models. For PINNs, recent work has introduced Bayesian PINNs (Zou & Karniadakis, [2025](#page-26-23)) and ensemble‐based PINNs (Soibam et al., [2024\)](#page-25-29) to estimate predictive uncertainty. These additions would enhance the framework's robustness and support more realistic applications.

#### **5.3.2. Outlook and Future Directions**

Traditional implementations of ML in ESMs often focus on developing global emulators (Alizadeh et al., [2020](#page-22-12); Price et al., [2025](#page-25-30); Ullrich et al., [2025](#page-26-24)) or tuning model parameters(Feng, Liu et al., [2022\)](#page-23-30). However, an alternative approach is to leverage ML at targeted local scales where traditional ESM components face the greatest challenges. Instead of a single ML model for an entire domain, multiple specialized ML models can be deployed at sensitive regions, such as river deltas, coastal estuaries, and major floodplains or even a small portion of them. These models can be modularly integrated within the ESM framework, interfacing through a shared API or data‐ exchange layer to provide higher‐resolution physics only where needed. This design improves ESM performance where needed while avoiding unnecessary computational overhead elsewhere. Such an approach offers a scalable adaptive solution for enhancing flood modeling within ESMs, bridging the gap between coarse global ESM simulations and local hydrodynamic accuracy under increasing hydrometeorological extremes.

Within this broader vision, the specific bottleneck our study addresses is the integration of high‐resolution flood dynamics into ESMs, which cannot practically operate at the resolutions required to capture CF at river–ocean interfaces. CF is used here as a representative example of the resolution mismatch between coarse river and fine ocean components (see Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1 and Section [3.1\)](#page-7-0). High‐resolution numerical models could, in principle, resolve these dynamics locally, but their setup demands substantial computational resources, specialized expertise, and repeated effort if applied across many regions globally. In contrast, ESMs are already configured for global coverage and include processes such as surface hydrology, biogeochemistry, and sediment transport, making selective downscaling a more practical approach for improving performance at critical interfaces. Purely data‐driven surrogates such as CNN‐LSTM also depend on high‐resolution model output for pretraining, requiring the same costly setup. PINNs, by contrast, function as physics‐constrained PDE solvers that require only boundary conditions from coarse ESMs and optional observational data. They also provide a natural framework for assimilating sparse observations in space and time while preserving physical consistency (see Figures [12](#page-19-0) and [13](#page-19-1) and Section [5.2.1\)](#page-18-0). This unique capability, difficult to achieve with purely data‐driven surrogates, marks the potential role of FD‐PINN in future operational systems.

# **6. Conclusions**

This study evaluates physics‐informed and data‐driven neural networks for simulating compound flooding at the river‐ocean interface, with the broader goal of enhancing large‐scale ESM frameworks for more accurate simulations in locally challenging regions. A finite‐difference‐based PINN (FD‐PINN) was developed to enhance computational efficiency, achieving a 6.5 × speedup over standard PINN while maintaining accuracy. Additionally, a new data‐generation approach was introduced to ensure robust training data sets for data‐driven models. Comparative analysis reveals that while the fully connected CNN (CNN‐FC) achieves the best overall performance, it comes at a high computational cost. The hybrid CNN‐LSTM, along with LSTM and GRU, provides a favorable balance between accuracy and generalization, particularly for unseen events. In contrast, CNN‐Conv and U‐Net show limited generalization. These findings highlight the trade‐offs between physics‐ informed and purely data‐driven methods, demonstrating the potential of integrating adaptive ML tools in ESM frameworks for improving extreme event modeling at local regions. Future work should explore integrating satellite observations and extending PINNs to higher‐dimensional hydrodynamic simulations, further enhancing predictive capabilities in climate‐driven hydroclimatic risk assessments.
