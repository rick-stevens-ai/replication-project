194 Training Data

19s The functional form of the Tersoff potential includes several
196 parameters that need to be trained to reproduce experimental or
197 high-fidelity computational data. Figure 1 illustrates the most stable
198 bismuthene polymorph, whose structure and other properties were
199 utilized for fitting the Tersoff potential parameters. The most stable
200 bismuthene polymorph identified in this work corresponds to a low-
201 buckled honeycomb structure. The optimized geometry is charac-
202 terized by a two-atom primitive cell with hexagonal symmetry, a
203 nearest-neighbor Bi—Bi bond length of approximately 3 A, and an
204 out-of-plane buckling height of about 1.7 A. The lattice constants are
205 equal along the in-plane directions, consistent with a two-dimensional
206 hexagonal lattice. This buckling stabilizes the structure by promoting
207 sp*-like hybridization. Although previous literature reports various
208 polymorphs of bismuthene, including symmetric washboard, asym-
209 metric washboard, and flat structures, the low-buckled polymorph is
210 noted for having the lowest cohesive energy and highest stability.**
211 Therefore, it was selected for training the model developed in this
212 study. DFT was used to determine the lattice parameters, cohesive
213 energy, elastic constants, and phonon dispersion properties of
214 bismuthene which served as the target properties for the RL-agent
218 to optimize the Tersoff interatomic potential parameters. DFT

calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation 216
Package (VASP)** utilizing the projector augmented wave (PAW)** 217
method. In the data generation process, we employed the Perdew— 218
Burke—Ernzerhof (PBE)*° functional form with a plane wave energy 219
cutoff of 500 eV to ensure a balance between computational efficiency 220
and accuracy. The tolerances for our calculations, was set at 10° and 221
10° eV for electronic and ionic convergence, respectively. A T- 222
centered k-point mesh with a sampling density of 0.035A7'was used. 223
To compute the elasticity tensor, the finite lattice distortions of 0.015 224
A were employed. This allowed us to extract the elastic constants, 225
denoted as Cij, through stress-strain analysis. For the phonon 226
computation, we calculated the second order force constants and 227
phonon dispersion using the finite difference method with the 228
Phonopy tool*” and VASP’S Density Functional Perturbation Theory 229
(DFPT).** For calculating the dynamical matrix which represents 230
how the atoms in the crystal respond to small atomic displacements, a 231
supercell configuration of 6 X 6 X 1, in conjunction with a 4 x 4 x 1 232

k-point grid was chosen. 233
Multireward Reinforcement Learning for Parameter 234
Search 235

The Tersoff potential parameters were optimized using the reinforce- 236
ment learning based workflow where the continuous Monte Carlo 237
Tree Search Algorithm (c-MCTS) is integrated with hierarchical 238
rewards. The c-MCTS workflow has 4 stages: selection, expansion, 239
simulation, and backpropagation. Each node is denoted by a 13 240
dimensional vector which corresponds to the parameters of the 241
Tersoff potential. The reward of the algorithm is evaluated 242
hierarchically and decided based on the closeness of the predicted 243
property as compared to the target property for a parameter set (see 244
next section for details). In the selection stage, a child node is selected 245
from a parent node and this selection is governed by the Upper 246
Confidence Bound (UCB) score (see eq 8). In the expansion stage, 247
the node with the least score is selected and in the simulation stage, 248
simluations or playouts are executed around that node to search the 249
parameter space effectively. In the backpropagation stage, the score 250
obtained in the simulations are updated backward throughout the 251
tree. Effective sampling of the parameter space is done through two 252
ways: adaptive sampling and window scaling scheme. In the adaptive 253
sampling scheme, the parameters are chosen such that the distance 254

266 In this study, we started with $0 nodes and expanded from there to
267 4 child nodes per parent node and executed S$ playouts per child node.
268 The depth of the tree was chosen as 7 and the exploration constant as
269 80. In this problem, we used the hierarchical objective function to
270 decide the rewards based on the closeness of the predicted properties
271 to the target properties. The chosen target properties were lattice
272 constants, cohesive energy, equation of state, elastic constants, and
273 phonon. By meaning of hierarchy we meant that first the crystal lattice
274 structure was fitted and only if the predicted lattice was less than a
278 certain error tolerance as compared to the target lattice the optimizer
276 moved to the next step of fitting to the cohesive energy followed by
277 the equation of state, elastic constant, and phonon dispersion curve.
278 The hierarchy was decided based on the importance of the properties.
279 To illustrate, it is very essential for the optimized potential parameter
280 to capture the crystal lattice structure and the cohesive energy
281 correctly and then only it could model the other properties like elastic
282 constants and phonon that are dependent on these fundamental
283 properties. The error tolerance for the crystal lattice structure was set
284 at 3%, cohesive energy as 3%, shape of the equation of state curve as
285 30%, elastic constant as 30%, and the shape of phonon dispersion
286 curve as 30%.

287 The errors in the crystal lattice structure and cohesive energy were
288 calculated with respect to reference DFT values. For the equation of
289 state (EOS), the error is evaluated by comparing the EOS curves
290 obtained from reference DFT data and from the fitted Tersoff
291 potential. Two metrics were used to quantify the EOS error. First, a
292 shape error measures how well the fitted potential reproduces the
293 overall curvature of the EOS curve after aligning the energy minima,
294 ensuring that the elastic response is captured correctly rather than
298 only the equilibrium point. Second, a shift error measures the
296 difference in the equilibrium (minimum) energy between the
297 reference and fitted curves, penalizing errors in cohesive energy.
298 The total EOS error is obtained as a weighted combination of these
299 two contributions.

300 Phonon dispersion errors are evaluated by directly comparing
301 phonon frequencies from the fitted potential with reference data along
302 identical high-symmetry paths in the Brillouin zone. Several terms
303 were used to quantify the error. A stability-related term penalizes the
304 presence of additional imaginary phonon modes, ensuring that the
308 fitted potential does not introduce unphysical lattice instabilities. A
306 frequency gap term compares characteristic gaps between acoustic
307 and optical branches. A band-shape term measures deviations
308 between predicted phonon branches with reference to DFT
309 calculation across the Brillouin zone, with greater emphasis on low-
310 frequency acoustic modes that dominate elastic and thermal behavior.
311 Additional terms control the maximum phonon frequencies to avoid
312 overstiffening, and explicitly evaluate errors at the I’ point to preserve
313 correct acoustic behavior and symmetry. The total phonon error is
314 computed as a weighted combination of all these contributions.

Properly defining rewards in a RL based search is essential for
guiding the agent toward optimal outcomes. A well-designed reward
function ensures that the agent’s actions are aligned with the desired
objectives by providing clear feedback on performance. This
alignment helps the agent focus on strategies that lead to better
results, minimizing the exploration of less relevant paths. Effective

rewards also facilitate a balance between exploration and exploitation, 3
enabling the agent to discover new solutions while refining known 35

effective strategies. In this case, the rewards an RL agent can earn in

the tree-based search depend on how well the properties predicted by 353

the RL-optimized Tersoff potential parameters align with respect to

enables the lattice vectors to adjust and redistribute internal
stress as the buckling height decreases, thereby lowering the
overall energy. When this relaxation pathway is blocked, the
structure accumulates significantly larger internal stresses,
resulting in a higher potential energy at each displacement
step.

sp’-like and sp*-like bonding configurations. None of the three

optimized standard Tersoff formulation can capture key
structural, mechanical, and vibrational properties of bismu-
thene, further improvements could be achieved by refining the
angular and cutoff terms or by adopting extended Tersoff-like
formulations that offer greater flexibility. Finally, we note that
approaches beyond fixed functional forms, such as data-driven
or symbolic regression—based** discovery of interaction terms,
represent promising future directions for overcoming intrinsic
limitations of predefined potential forms.
