   Do Leray–Hopf solutions, perhaps augmented with suitability, constitute an ex-
istence and uniqueness class for the Navier–Stokes equations? Since Leray’s work,
this has remained an important open problem in the theory of the Navier-Stokes
equations, as discussed in [30, 37] and, in the past decade, [33] and [11, Problem 9].
In 1969, Ladyzhenskaya [37] already gave an example of non-uniqueness to (NS),
though in a time-varying domain which degenerates as t → 0+ and with non-
standard boundary conditions and a force. In recent years, this problem has been
revisited, and various works have influenced the community toward the expectation
that uniqueness does not hold. We mention two of particular importance: First,
Jia, Šverák, and Guillod [32, 33, 28] developed a program towards non-uniqueness
and supported the missing steps with numerical evidence. Second, Buckmaster
and Vicol [12] constructed non-unique distributional solutions of the Navier-Stokes
equations with finite kinetic energy via the powerful method of convex integration.
Despite several attempts and results obtained alongside these ideas, convex in-
tegration is at the present time far from reaching the minimum regularity (1.1)
required to speak of Leray-Hopf solutions.
   In this paper, we answer the uniqueness question in the negative:
Theorem 1.2 (Non-uniqueness). There exist T > 0, f ∈ L1t L2x (R3+ × (0, T )), and
two distinct suitable Leray–Hopf solutions u, ū to the Navier–Stokes equations on
R3 × (0, T ) with body force f and initial condition u0 ≡ 0.

  The solutions exhibited in this theorem are smooth for positive times and satisfy
many additional properties, which we describe in Theorem 1.3. It is elementary to
extend the distinct solutions to global-in-time Leray–Hopf solutions by modifying
the force after T and using Leray’s result.

First of all, there is no general tool to construct unstable solutions. Rather, unstable
solutions are typically sought among explicit solutions with many symmetries and
subsequently analyzed by ODE techniques. In three dimensions, the most natural
explicit steady states are shear flows and vortex columns, which do not decay and
hence have infinite energy. Remarkably, to our knowledge, there is no existing
unstable solution in three dimensions suitable for our purposes, with or without
forcing.

However, it is likely that our instability does break the following nearly symmetric

singularity are separate issues, though in models exhibiting both, the two issues

1.2. Comparison with existing literature. We now review the above cited
literature more in detail and point out certain connections with the present work.
   Self-similarity, instability, and non-uniqueness in the Navier-Stokes equations:
Jia, Šverák, and Guillod’s program [32, 33, 28]. Let a0 ∈ C ∞ (R3 \ {0}) be di-
vergence free and scaling invariant and σ ∈ R be a size parameter. One may ask
whether there exists a scaling invariant solution uσ with initial data u0,σ = σa0 . For
|σ| ≪ 1, existence and uniqueness falls into the known perturbation theory of Koch
and Tataru [35] in BMO−1 , for example. For arbitrary size, existence was demon-
strated in [32] via a new local-in-space smoothing estimate and Leray–Schauder
degree theory (see [5] and the references it cites for a simplified approach). Thus,
the self-similar solutions constructed in [32] with initial data u0,σ constitute a
continuous-in-σ branch connected to the origin, and non-uniqueness in the Cauchy
problem for (NS) is conjectured in [33] to arise due to bifurcations within or from
                                                                                     (σ)
this class of solutions.6 In [33], spectral conditions on the linearized operator Lss
(that is, linearized around the profiles Uσ ) are identified for such bifurcations to
occur. Moreover, it is shown how to truncate the solutions to have finite energy
without introducing a force (for this, it is roughly necessary to ‘watch’ the eigen-
value become unstable). One of the spectral conditions was verified numerically
in [28] on certain axisymmetric examples with pure swirl initial datum.
   One of the main difficulties in rigorous verification of the spectral condition
in [33] is that the self-similar solutions are non-explicit. In our work, the inclusion
of a force gives us the freedom to search for a more explicit unstable profile.

how generic this instability is and to develop a general method for finding these
unstable (forced) solutions. It is, moreover, an open problem to remove the force.

       2. Linear instability: from two dimensions to axisymmetry
2.1. Spectral preliminaries. Let H be a Hilbert space and L : D(L) ⊂ H → H
be closed and densely defined. Then D(L) is a Hilbert space with the graph norm.
The basic theory of these operators is reviewed in [41, VIII: Unbounded Operators].
A simple criterion for self-adjointness is that ker L + i and ker L − i are trivial.
   The resolvent set ρ(L) consists of those λ ∈ C such that λ − L : D(L) → H
is invertible. Its inverse R(λ, L) : H → D(L) is known as the resolvent, and it
is bounded by the open mapping theorem. The spectrum σ(L) is the complement
of the resolvent set. In our convention, the essential spectrum σess (L) is the set
of spectral values for which λ − L is not Fredholm of index zero.7 The essential
spectrum is closed. Moreover, [34, Theorem 5.28] (see also the discussion in [34,
Chapter 4, Section 6]) can be used to show that, in each connected component of
C \ σess (L), (i) λ − L is not invertible everywhere, or (ii) λ − L is invertible except
at isolated points.
   An operator T : D(L) → H is relatively compact with respect to L if T is
compact when D(L) is considered with the graph norm. A key property is that
relatively compact perturbations do not disturb the essential spectrum: σess (L +
T ) = σess (L), see [34, Theorem 5.26].
   We frequently use the following reasoning: Consider an operator L as above
whose essential spectrum is known to be contained in a half space {Re λ ≤ µ}. We
perturb L by a relatively compact perturbation T , so that σess (L + T ) = σess (L).
Furthermore, we typically know that the perturbed operator L + T is invertible
for Re λ ≫ 1. Hence, the spectrum in {Re λ > µ} consists of isolated points.
