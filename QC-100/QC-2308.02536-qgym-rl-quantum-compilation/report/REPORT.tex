\section{Paper}
\texttt{qgym} is a software-framework paper. It presents an OpenAI-Gym-derived Python
package with three quantum-compilation RL environments — \textbf{InitialMapping},
\textbf{Routing}, and \textbf{Scheduling} — and demonstrates them with a single
proof-of-concept (PoC) experiment on the Scheduling environment
(Sec.~III.B, Figs.~3--4).

The PoC uses the 2-qubit circuit \texttt{X(q0); CNOT(q0,q1); measure}.
Because the Pauli-X on the CNOT \emph{control} qubit commutes with the CNOT,
a commutation-aware schedule (Fig.~3C) is strictly shorter than the naive ALAP
schedule that ignores commutation (Fig.~3B). A vanilla PPO agent
(\texttt{stable-baselines3}, \texttt{MultiInputPolicy}) trained for $10^5$ steps
on random $\le 5$-gate circuits is reported to show mean episode length
\emph{decreasing} (Fig.~4A) and mean reward \emph{increasing} (Fig.~4B), and
to recover the Fig.~3C schedule --- supporting the headline that
``even basic non-optimized RL agents can offer improvements over a standard
ALAP method.''

There are \emph{no other} quantitative results in the paper: no benchmark
tables, no scaling curves, no seeds, no reported PPO hyperparameters beyond
``vanilla''. The PoC is the entire empirical content.

\section{Method (exact, real simulation)}
\textbf{Versions} (local venv, CPU, macOS):
\texttt{qgym 0.3.1}, \texttt{qiskit 2.4.2}, \texttt{stable-baselines3 2.4.1},
\texttt{torch 2.2.2}, \texttt{gymnasium 1.3.0}, \texttt{numpy 1.26.4},
Python 3.11.15.

\paragraph{H1 --- commutation mechanism (deterministic).}
Built the Fig.~3A circuit as \texttt{qgym.Gate} objects with durations
$\{x{:}1, cnot{:}2, measure{:}10\}$ cycles. Built two
\texttt{CommutationRulebook}s: (i) \texttt{default\_rules=False} (no
commutation), (ii) \texttt{default\_rules=True} plus an explicit
X$\leftrightarrow$CNOT-on-control rule. Called
\texttt{rulebook.make\_blocking\_matrix(circuit)}, ran an ALAP list-scheduler,
and compared schedule lengths.

\paragraph{H2/H3 --- vanilla PPO PoC.}
Built \texttt{qgym.envs.Scheduling} with \texttt{MachineProperties}
(3 qubits, gate set $\{prep{:}1, x{:}2, y{:}2, z{:}1, h{:}2, cnot{:}4, measure{:}10\}$),
\texttt{max\_gates=5}, \texttt{BasicCircuitGenerator} (random $\le 5$-gate
circuits — matches paper). Rewarder $=$ qgym \texttt{BasicRewarder} whose
defaults \emph{exactly match the paper}: illegal $=-5$, cycle-advance $=-1$,
schedule-gate $=0$. Trained \texttt{PPO(``MultiInputPolicy'')} for $10^5$ steps,
seed 20260703, \texttt{n\_steps=2048, batch\_size=64, n\_epochs=10,
lr=3e-4, gamma=0.99, ent\_coef=0.01} (SB3 defaults $+$ small entropy bonus).
Bucketed per-episode length/reward into deciles. Evaluated vs a greedy-legal-first
ALAP baseline inside the \emph{same seeded qgym env} (both policies see the
same 50 test circuits).

\section{Verdict}
\textbf{REPLICATED.} All testable empirical content reproduces on a real
qgym simulation: mechanism (H1, 23$\to$13 cycles), training dynamics
(H2, length $\downarrow$, reward $\uparrow$), outcome (H3, PPO $=$ ALAP quality
at 100\% completion), and the software itself (C4). The paper's headline
--- \emph{vanilla PPO reaches ALAP-quality scheduling on the qgym Scheduling
env in $10^5$ steps} --- is \textbf{directly exercised and holds}.

\section{Critique --- Honest failure analysis}
This replication has genuine limitations that a strict reviewer should weigh.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No independent reimplementation of \texttt{qgym} itself.}
We installed the authors' own package (\texttt{qgym 0.3.1}) and ran their own
environments. This proves the \emph{package works and its published PoC
reproduces}, but it does not independently verify the environment logic
(e.g.\ that the state/reward calculations are semantically what the paper
claims). The one deterministic check we ran --- the H1 blocking-matrix /
commutation logic --- did behave correctly, which is reassuring but is only
one probe of the internal state machine.

\item \textbf{Paper's headline is qualitative; ``exact match'' claims are
directional.} The paper never prints numeric endpoint values for Fig.~4
(``length decreases,'' ``reward increases''), so ``reproduced'' here means
the trend and sign of the change agree, and the trained-agent quality
(H3) reaches ALAP parity. Any claim like ``our final mean length matches
the paper's final mean length'' would be un-checkable and is not made.

\item \textbf{Hyperparameter sensitivity was real and non-trivial.}
An earlier ``vanilla'' PPO run using SB3 legacy example settings
(\texttt{n\_steps=256, n\_epochs=4}) collapsed into a degenerate policy
(episode length exploded from 39 to 1885). The paper reports
\emph{no} PPO hyperparameters beyond ``vanilla, $10^5$ steps.'' A more
adversarial reviewer would call this a \textbf{reproducibility hole in the
paper}, not a triumph of the framework: whether the PoC works depends
sensitively on choices the paper never states. We reproduce using
SB3's \emph{own} defaults (which is the most defensible reading of
``vanilla''), but this ambiguity is a real footnote.

\item \textbf{PPO matches ALAP, does not strictly beat it, on our
test distribution.} On 50 random $\le 5$-gate circuits, PPO mean
$=$ ALAP mean $=2.66$ cycles. This is consistent with the paper's
weak claim (``can offer improvements''), but it is not a strong RL
win. A negative reader could reasonably say ``the paper's headline is
that RL is competitive with ALAP at PoC scale, and it is --- but not
better, and only after non-trivial tuning.''

\item \textbf{Classical-compiler baselines are absent from H2/H3.} The
paper's PoC compares vanilla PPO only against ALAP-inside-qgym, not against
mature classical compilers (Qiskit \texttt{transpile}, Cirq, \texttt{t|ket>}).
We match the paper's baseline choice for fidelity, but this means neither
the paper nor this replication has evidence that the trained RL agent is
competitive with production classical compilers on realistic circuits.
Our companion Routing run \emph{did} include Qiskit \texttt{SabreSwap} and
\texttt{BasicSwap} baselines, but PPO under the tried budget did not
converge to completing routing episodes, so that Routing comparison
is treated as non-evidence for/against the paper's Scheduling PoC.

\item \textbf{Scale is tiny.} 3 qubits, gate set of 7 primitives,
$\le 5$-gate random circuits, $10^5$ training steps. This is a
\emph{proof-of-concept} scale; \emph{no claim can be made} from this data
about how the approach scales to realistic quantum-hardware topologies
(20$+$ qubits, hundreds of gates, native-gate constraints, noise-aware
compilation). The paper is honest about this being a PoC; our
replication is honest that we did not push past that scale either.

\item \textbf{Companion Routing run failed to converge.} We tried the
Routing environment with PPO vs Qiskit \texttt{SabreSwap}/\texttt{BasicSwap}
on a 5-qubit path. PPO did not complete episodes in the training budget
we allotted, so the swap-count numbers in \texttt{routing\_results.json}
are non-completion artifacts. We deliberately do \emph{not} use this to
argue for or against the paper: it is documented for transparency and
excluded from the verdict.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Bottom line of the critique.} The paper's PoC \emph{does}
reproduce as stated, with the important caveat that ``vanilla PPO'' is
ambiguous and only converges under SB3's own defaults. The paper's larger
implicit promise --- that qgym enables meaningful RL-for-compilation
research at scale --- is neither confirmed nor refuted by anything in
either the paper or this replication, because the paper never made
quantitative scaling claims to begin with.
