\begin{abstract}
We independently replicate the central claim of Shaydulin \& Wild (arXiv:2111.05451,
Phys.\ Rev.\ Research 4, 043017 (2022)) that the \emph{bandwidth} hyperparameter
$\lambda$ of a quantum-embedding kernel controls a non-monotonic trade-off between
kernel over-concentration (near-orthogonal states, $K\!\approx\!0$) and kernel
under-informativeness (nearly-identity, $K\!\approx\!1$), with a sweet-spot
bandwidth that makes the resulting quantum-kernel SVC competitive with a classical
RBF baseline. Using PennyLane statevector simulation of a 4-qubit IQP feature map
on \texttt{make\_moons}, averaged over 5 seeds, we reproduce (i) the collapse to
random-guess accuracy ($0.510 \pm 0.052$) at large $\lambda$, (ii) the classical-competitive
optimum ($0.820$ vs.\ classical RBF $0.875$) at $\lambda=0.3$, and (iii) the monotone
off-diagonal-kernel decay from $0.9992 \to 0.065$ that drives the effect. Verdict:
\textbf{REPLICATED}. Caveats: the paper's headline is a scaling claim in qubit
count; we exercise only the bandwidth mechanism at fixed $n\!=\!4$, and we use
\texttt{make\_moons} rather than the paper's Fashion-MNIST / KMNIST / PLASTiCC
datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Paper summary}

Shaydulin \& Wild introduce a bandwidth hyperparameter $\lambda$ for quantum kernel
methods by rescaling inputs $x_i \leftarrow \lambda x_i$ inside a quantum feature
map. Because the fidelity kernel $k(x_i, x_j) = |\langle \phi(x_j) | \phi(x_i) \rangle|^2$
exponentially concentrates around $0$ as the number of qubits grows for a fixed
feature map --- the ``no-go for quantum kernels'' of Huang et al.\ and K\"ubler
et al.\ --- $\lambda$ controls a fundamental trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\lambda$ too \emph{large}: feature-map angles are large, quantum states are
    nearly orthogonal, $k \approx 0$ off-diagonal, SVC memorizes training points and
    generalizes at chance level;
  \item $\lambda$ too \emph{small}: feature-map angles $\approx 0$, all states
    $\approx H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle$, $k \approx 1$ everywhere, kernel carries no
    information;
  \item \emph{intermediate} $\lambda$: best generalization, and can restore the
    performance-vs-qubit-count scaling of the quantum-kernel SVC from
    \emph{degrading} to \emph{improving}, becoming competitive with the best
    classical baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}

\textbf{Environment.} Python 3 venv, \texttt{pennylane 0.45.1} (statevector
\texttt{default.qubit}, exact simulation, no shots), \texttt{scikit-learn 1.9.0}
(\texttt{SVC(kernel='precomputed', C=1.0)}), \texttt{numpy 2.5.0}. Host: CherryRd
(macOS Darwin 25.3.0), CPU only. Runtime $\sim$4\,s single-seed, $\sim$25\,s multi-seed.

\textbf{Data.} \texttt{sklearn.datasets.make\_moons($n$=80, noise=0.20)}, standardized
to mean 0 / std 1, deterministically lifted from 2D to 4D via
$[x_1, x_2, \sin x_1, \cos x_2]$ then re-standardized. 40/40 stratified train/test
split, \texttt{random\_state = seed}.

\textbf{Feature map (IQP-style, Eq.\ 5).} Depth $r\!=\!2$:
\[
U(x) = \prod_{r=1}^{2} H^{\otimes n}\;
       \exp\!\Big[\, i\!\Big(\sum_j \lambda x_j Z_j
       + \sum_{j<k} \lambda^2 x_j x_k Z_j Z_k \Big) \Big].
\]
Implemented as \texttt{RZ($-2\lambda x_j$)} single-qubit phases and
\texttt{IsingZZ($-2\lambda^2 x_j x_k$)} for all pairs.

\textbf{Kernel + classifier.} Statevector $|\phi(x)\rangle$ computed exactly via
\texttt{qml.state()}. Fidelity kernel $K[i,j] = |\langle \phi(X_2[j]) | \phi(X_1[i]) \rangle|^2$
from batched inner products. \texttt{SVC(kernel='precomputed', C=1.0)} --- same
family as the paper.

\textbf{Bandwidth grid.} $\lambda \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0\}$;
7 values $\times$ 5 seeds = 35 SVC fits.

\section{Critique --- honest assessment of what was actually shown}

This section is deliberately harsher than the top-line verdict to make the
scope of the replication explicit.

\paragraph{What was independently reimplemented + reproduced.}
The bandwidth-vs-accuracy \emph{curve shape} was reimplemented from Eq.\ 5 of the
paper (IQP feature map + $\lambda$ input scaling), run on independent PennyLane
statevector simulation (no code copied from the authors' repo), and produced
qualitatively and quantitatively the same non-monotone shape --- collapse to
$\approx 0.5$ at large $\lambda$, sweet spot at intermediate $\lambda$,
mild under-fitting at small $\lambda$. The mechanism claim (off-diagonal $K$
monotone in $\lambda$) is also reproduced quantitatively.

\paragraph{What was NOT reproduced on the paper's specific setting.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Datasets.} The paper's headline plots (Fig.\ 2) use Fashion-MNIST,
    KMNIST, and PLASTiCC PCA-reduced to $n$ qubits. We used only
    \texttt{make\_moons} (a synthetic 2D toy). We assert but do not \emph{prove}
    dataset-independence.
  \item \textbf{Qubit-scaling.} Fig.\ 2's central claim is that optimized $\lambda$
    keeps the quantum-kernel-SVC accuracy \emph{improving} with $n$ up to
    $\sim$14 qubits. We did not sweep $n$; we tested only $n\!=\!4$. The
    \emph{practical-utility} extrapolation of the paper is therefore untested.
  \item \textbf{Feature-map breadth.} We tested only IQP-style. The paper claims
    the bandwidth effect holds across at least three feature maps
    (IQP, Hamiltonian evolution, and a variational ansatz).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Classical-baseline comparison --- weak by design.}
We compared to classical RBF and linear SVM on the same 40/40 \texttt{make\_moons}
split. Both classical baselines score $0.875$; the quantum sweet-spot scores
$0.820$. On \texttt{make\_moons}, the quantum kernel is therefore
\emph{competitive-but-worse} at $n\!=\!4$. This is consistent with the paper
(which argues the quantum advantage only shows up as $n$ grows) but it does NOT
independently corroborate the paper's implicit quantum-advantage story. To
corroborate that, one would need $n \in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$ sweeps and side-by-side
classical baselines --- outside our compute budget.

\paragraph{Concentration/trainability trade-off --- verified qualitatively, not tied to a Frobenius-norm concentration metric.}
The paper connects the small-bandwidth regime to \emph{kernel concentration}
in the sense of Huang et al./K\"ubler et al. Our diagnostic (off-diagonal $K$ mean)
is a proxy: it collapses monotonically from $0.9992$ to $0.065$. We did NOT
compute the formal ``kernel concentration'' quantity
$\mathrm{Var}_{x,x'}[k(x,x')]$ over a data measure, nor the sample-complexity
lower bound implied by it. The mechanism is corroborated; the formal
statistical-learning-theory link is asserted, not re-derived.

\paragraph{Single-seed count and $C$ hyperparameter.}
We ran 5 seeds. The standard deviations at the collapse point ($0.052$) and at
the optimum ($0.027$) are tight enough to distinguish random-guess from optimum
with high confidence, but a 20-seed sweep would strengthen the statistics for
the more marginal $\lambda \in \{0.05, 0.10, 1.0\}$ regimes. The SVC regularization
parameter $C\!=\!1.0$ was NOT tuned per-$\lambda$; the paper's headline is
robust to $C$ but a strict replication would sweep $C$ as well.

\paragraph{LLM-judge fallback.}
The Argo Opus 4.7 endpoint returned a validation error for the judge prompt;
we fell back to Argo GPT-5.2 (also free). This is a single-judge check --- fine
at this evidence strength (multi-seed collapse to $0.510 \pm 0.052$ is
unambiguous) but not a cross-model consensus.

\section{Verdict}

\textbf{REPLICATED} for the paper's central mechanism (C1--C4) on a small
independent implementation. NOT REPLICATED for the qubit-scaling claim (C5)
or the paper's specific datasets (C6). The paper's \emph{practical} claim
(quantum-kernel SVC can be competitive with best classical, at scale) is
consistent with our small-$n$ result but is not independently corroborated
here.
